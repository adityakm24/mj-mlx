# Forecast-driven expert paging leads the ranking

The strongest candidate for a general, exact, model-agnostic systems idea in mj-mlx is on the streamed path: treat experts as pages and let a routing forecast drive both prefetch and eviction, while the model's real router still decides what executes. The project's own traces make the case better than any paper: on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B about 14 ms of each roughly 48 ms decode step is SSD wait, 55% of discarded speculative passes had the right prediction but a late read, and the offline optimum halves LRU's misses (5.2% against 11.1%) "only from knowing future routing" ([RESULTS 1s, 1u, 1v](../RESULTS.md)). Outside work now supplies what the project's rejected linear predictor lacked: next-layer predictors at 93-97% on Qwen3-30B against the project's 80-83%, trained on ten to a thousand times more tokens. In-memory decode sits at 98% of the bandwidth floor ([RESULTS 1i](../RESULTS.md)), so the only lever left there is tokens per pass, and the project's exact speculative decoding (2.0-2.1x on chat) has to move inside the serving scheduler with a cost table that prices verify rows in joules and in expert rows. The project also holds a lead nobody contests, because no Mac engine publishes energy per token, but every number it has is the GPU rail, so whole-machine interleaved energy is the cheapest high-value item on the list. Two cautions frame all of this: headline state of the art moved in 2026 to M5-class machines and to expert-streaming engines the project has not benchmarked (Splash, BaseRT, AiiStream, llama.cpp's expert cache and its disk-offload fork), and the evidence for the top idea is thin in one specific place, since no paper measures prediction more than one layer ahead on 2025-26 many-expert models.

## Three candidates for the project's PagedAttention

PagedAttention was a memory-management abstraction that sat under any transformer. For mixture-of-experts models on a memory-bound machine the analogous object is the expert set. The slot pool filled by positional reads is already common property: the llama.cpp Metal proof of concept, ds4, Routide, TurboFieldfare and Tideway all use it ([llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324), [ds4 issue #638](https://github.com/antirez/ds4/issues/638), [Tideway](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14848v1)). A name therefore has to attach to what is new. Directions 1 and 8 together form one abstraction, expert virtual memory whose pager is fed by a forecast and whose indirection never leaves the GPU timeline. Direction 3 is a policy layer rather than a memory structure, but it is the one where the project already has data nobody else has. The closest published neighbour is Tideway (10 November 2026), which is exact, manages residency across VRAM and RAM on a desktop GPU, and states that it uses no lookahead prediction ([Tideway](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14848v1)); the two strongest SSD results, Edge0 and CECAR, change the routing rather than forecast it ([Edge0](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18063v2), [CECAR](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/152287)). Exact, forecast-driven paging on unified memory is unoccupied.

### 1. Forecast paging for experts

**Idea.** Keep the slot pools, and make a routing forecast a first-class input to one pager. The forecast can come from any source (later layers' routers applied to a corrected hidden state, a small per-layer predictor, the routing seen in speculative verify rows) and is used twice: to issue reads early, and to protect forecast experts from eviction so that LRU approaches the offline optimum. The true router always decides what runs, so a wrong forecast costs one demand read and nothing else.

**Gap it addresses.** With the C++ streamer and keep-alive the GPU is busy 63.5% of a Qwen3.6-35B decode step; 12.3 SSD misses per token cost about 14 ms of read wait; 760 of 1,386 discarded speculative passes (55%) had the prediction right but the read not landed; the project's own next-layer prediction is 80-83% at depth 1, 74-77% at depth 2 and 70-73% at depth 3 ([RESULTS 1s, 1u](../RESULTS.md)). Offline on 8,192 tokens, LRU misses 11.1% of expert accesses at 96 slots, LFU with decay 10.3-10.7%, and the optimum 5.2% ([RESULTS 1v](../RESULTS.md)). Going from 96 to 128 slots is 11-12% faster but fails on 16 GB ([RESULTS 1s, 1w](../RESULTS.md)); forecast paging tries to buy that hit rate without the memory.

**Outside evidence.** Training-free residual correction of the gate input reaches 93.3% prefetch accuracy on Qwen3-30B-A3B against 77.6% for the HybriMoE baseline, and in the same system workload-aware replacement is worth about 38% while prefetching alone is worth about 9% (RTX 3090 with experts in host RAM) ([DALI](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03495)). A per-layer predictor trained offline on 128 batches of 512 tokens for about 30 epochs reaches 97% top-4 hits on Qwen3-30B-A3B ([PreScope](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23638v1)). Two linear functions on the activations before the attention block reach 94.69% on Qwen3-30B and buy an attention block of extra lead (abstract only) ([pre-attention prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10676v1)). An untrained copy of the next router scores 70-80% and rises to 87-94% after distillation on Qwen3-235B and gpt-oss-120b ([PROBE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00509v2)); that untrained band is the band the project measured. Under LRU 34.2% of evicted experts are needed again within five steps, against 0.1% under the optimum, while an eviction policy learned from recency and frequency alone adds only about 7% throughput on Qwen3-30B-A3B ([FlashMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17063v1)), which agrees with the project's LFU result. Using draft-token verification as a lookahead sensor for prefetch and eviction gave 25.06 against 17.66 tok/s over llama.cpp with speculative decoding (RTX 4090, Qwen3-30B-A3B) ([MoE-SpAc](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09983v1)). On a 16 GB M2 a next-token "prerouter" roughly doubles decode over on-demand streaming (1.8 to 3.3 tok/s at 8 active experts), but there the prediction replaces the routing, a recovery adapter is trained, and no standalone recall is reported ([Edge0](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18063v2)). The thin spot: no paper reports accuracy two or more layers ahead for the 2025-26 many-expert families; the roughly 90% figure for two to three layers ahead is for Mixtral and Phi-MoE with 8-16 experts ([HOBBIT](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01433v2)).

**Why this reopens a rejected experiment, and what is different.** The project closed a learned linear lookahead that overfit at about 6,000 training tokens and gained 0.7 points of recall ([RESULTS 1u](../RESULTS.md)). The published predictors that work were trained on about 65,000 tokens repeated for 30 epochs (128 x 512, my multiplication) up to 4-5 million tokens ([PreScope](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23638v1), [Speculating Experts](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19289v1)), and they use different inputs: pre-attention activations, offline residual vectors, or a residual network on top of a frozen router copy. The earlier test was under-trained by one to three orders of magnitude and used the weakest input. What stays closed: wider top-(k+N) prefetch, depth 3 with the raw router, and eviction from history alone ([RESULTS 1d.3, 1s, 1v](../RESULTS.md)).

**Exact and model-agnostic.** Exact by construction. Model-agnostic in its training-free forms; a trained predictor is an optional per-model add-on that leaves base weights untouched.

**Expected gain (estimate).** A step is 48.1-48.8 ms (20.5-20.8 tok/s at 96 slots). The optimum's miss ratio to LRU is 5.2 / 11.1, about 0.47. If SSD wait scales with misses, halving it saves about 7 ms and gives about 24 tok/s, +17%, which is what 128 slots gave (23.5-23.9 tok/s) without the memory. Removing all 14 ms bounds the gain near +40%. Where a real forecast lands between those depends on its horizon, which is the unknown the first experiment measures.

**Effort.** Days for the offline study; two to three weeks for per-layer budgets, forecast-protected eviction and a predictor hook in the streamer.

**First experiment.** CPU only, on the existing 8,192-token routing trace. First, a horizon-limited oracle: LRU at 96 slots with eviction protection for the true routing one layer, four layers, one token and two tokens ahead, set against 11.1%, 10.3-10.7% and 5.2%. This bounds what any forecast of that horizon can do before a predictor is built. Second, recall at depths 1-4 for the raw router, a residual-corrected router, and a pre-attention linear predictor trained on at least 65,000 tokens. Kill the eviction half if the one-token oracle does not beat LFU with decay; kill the predictor half if corrected or trained recall at depth 2-3 does not exceed the raw router's 80-83% at depth 1.

### 3. Priced speculation inside the scheduler

**Idea.** Make a multi-token decode item with rollback a scheduler primitive, and choose verify rows each step by expected accepted tokens over measured cost, where the cost table is in joules or seconds and includes two terms no published policy has: the marginal expert rows a draft pulls in, and on streamed models the expected SSD misses. Add a model-free drafter (suffix or prompt lookup) for models that ship no draft head.

**Gap it addresses.** Qwen3.5-9B runs at 46.3 ms against a 45.3 ms bandwidth floor ([RESULTS 1i](../RESULTS.md)). The model's own MTP head gives 22.08 / 22.80 ms per token against 46.46 / 46.66 plain at acceptance 0.82 ([RESULTS 1k](../RESULTS.md)), but the Rust scheduler has no multi-token items or rollback, so this lives outside the serving engine and is greedy only ([RESULTS 1n, 1u](../RESULTS.md)). On MoE each verify row costs experts: gpt-oss verify steps of 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 tokens take 32.3 / 43.9 / 56.2 / 70.3 ms ([RESULTS 1p](../RESULTS.md)), and on the streamed 35B the gain is +7-14% with about 17 demand misses per token ([RESULTS 1u, 1v](../RESULTS.md)). Energy does not follow time: a forward for 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 tokens takes 40.3 / 40.9 / 42.1 / 53.3 ms but 417 / 713 / 933 / 1050 mJ, and one draft pays for itself in energy only above about 0.58 acceptance ([RESULTS 1l](../RESULTS.md)). OLMoE, DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Qwen3-30B have no speculative path (checklist 3.4, [LAUNCH_TODO](../LAUNCH_TODO.md)).

**Outside evidence.** The measured-cost-table policy is the newest engine work: per-position confidences become survival probabilities and the number of verified slots maximises expected tokens per unit step time from tables profiled at startup ([vLLM, Aug 2026](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-08-14-dspark-adaptive-verification)); an online estimator needs no confidence head and is far better calibrated than raw draft confidence (calibration error 0.0100 against 0.0736) ([vLLM, Oct 2026](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-14-adaptive-verification-online-estimator)). Projected speedup from step time and acceptance length matched measurement (2.22x projected, 2.23x measured) ([SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)). For MoE, verification overhead is 2-3x and utility gating limits worst-case slowdown to 5% while adding 7-14% over a static draft length ([Cascade](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20675v1)); a per-layer expert budget over the draft tree adds 10-30% over EAGLE-3 but substitutes experts, so it is approximate ([MoE-Spec](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16052v1)). The project's own cache study noted an exact method that picks draft length by the new experts it pulls in, reported at 1.45x, and did not build it ([RESULTS 1v](../RESULTS.md)). Suffix-tree drafting needs no model and reports up to 5.3x on repetitive agent workloads (abstract) ([SuffixDecoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04975)), while n-gram drafting on general text is a slowdown at 0.88x ([SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)). Every engine that added MTP had to add recurrent-state rollback first ([llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673), [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)). All of these numbers are from server GPUs. No source reports energy per token for any speculative method, so a joule-denominated policy is an unoccupied claim.

**Exact and model-agnostic.** Greedy outputs are already verified token-identical ([RESULTS 1j, 1p, 1u](../RESULTS.md)). For sampling, the project's sampler draws Gumbel noise as a pure function of seed, position and vocabulary id ([sampler.py](../../src/mj_mlx/sampler.py)), so a draft token can be checked against the exact token plain sampling would produce at that position. This is my inference from the notes, not a measured result; a server engine shipped seeded rejection sampling for the same purpose in November 2026 ([SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)). The primitive and the policy are model-agnostic; draft heads are per model; the suffix drafter needs none.

**Expected gain.** Served Qwen3.5-9B chat moves from 1.0x to the already measured 1.80-1.89x at one request, 1.41-1.42x at two and 1.25-1.30x at four ([RESULTS 1n, 1o, 1r](../RESULTS.md)); gpt-oss gains about 1.15-1.2x ([RESULTS 1p, 1q](../RESULTS.md)). Any gain beyond those from expert-aware depth is unknown; the 1.45x figure is a reported number, not reproduced here.

**Effort.** Three to five weeks: multi-token items, rollback and state checkpoints in the scheduler; the cost table and per-depth acceptance already exist ([RESULTS 1l, 1n](../RESULTS.md)).

**First experiment.** Offline, from logged MTP runs on the streamed 35B: per step, count the new unique experts and demand misses each draft row pulled in against tokens accepted, and test whether any expert-aware depth rule beats fixed depth on the logs. Kill the streamed-model part if no rule beats depth 1. Separately, one interleaved run of sampled MTP against plain sampling with the same seed, checking token identity.

**Not re-proposed.** Pipelining the verify step. Server engines report gains from zero-overhead speculative scheduling ([vLLM Model Runner V2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2)), but the project measured eager pipelined verify 19-43% slower and deferred verify neutral because the graph build is not on its critical path ([RESULTS 1l, 1v](../RESULTS.md)), and nothing in the outside evidence contradicts that for this machine.

### 8. Optimistic expert indirection

**Idea.** Put the expert-to-slot table in a GPU buffer. Each layer's kernel resolves its routed experts through the table and writes a miss flag, and the step runs through consecutive layers with no host decision. The host checks flags asynchronously and, on a miss, replays from that layer once the read lands. The common all-hit layer stops paying a GPU-to-CPU-to-GPU round trip.

**Gap it addresses.** With keep-alive, about 5.8 ms per streamed step is idle in roughly 36 gaps of 100-300 µs, the per-layer event round trip near its floor; a real round trip costs 95-105 µs with a spinning CPU ([RESULTS 1u, 1v](../RESULTS.md)). Idle gaps also slow what follows: the same matvec takes 366 µs with no idle before it and 418 µs after 0.5 ms ([RESULTS 1u](../RESULTS.md)). The internal review concluded that removing the host decision for all-hit layers "would need a different mechanism than those tried".

**Outside evidence.** Rivals pay the same tax. In the llama.cpp Metal proof of concept a CPU step for slot remapping halved generation speed, and the redesigned shared-event handshake still costs about 21% with every slot resident (38.1 against 30 tok/s on an M3 Pro) ([llama.cpp issue #20757](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/20757), [llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324)). Public Metal lacks asynchronous copy (abstract only) ([Apple-silicon applicability study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31479)). No engine in the notes validates routing after the fact.

**What differs from the rejected attempt.** The project tried a GPU spin-wait on a CPU-written flag and found CPU writes are not visible to a running kernel ([RESULTS 1u, 1v](../RESULTS.md)). Here the GPU never waits on the CPU: the table changes only between command buffers and validation is deferred.

**Exact and model-agnostic.** Yes to both.

**Expected gain (estimate).** At most 5.8 ms of about 48.5 ms, 12%, if every round trip vanished. With 12.3 misses per token across 40 layers, at least about 28 layers per token are miss-free (my arithmetic, a lower bound because misses cluster), so about 4 ms or 8%, plus an unmeasured benefit from steadier GPU clocks. Miss layers get more expensive because queued work behind a miss is wasted, so this pays only after direction 1 lowers misses.

**Effort.** Two to four weeks in the C++ streamer and kernels; KV and state writes must be deferred or made safe to replay.

**First experiment.** Re-run the existing no-sync replay harness ([RESULTS 1d.3](../RESULTS.md)) on today's C++ path with the same prompt; the difference from the live step is the ceiling. Drop the idea if it is under about 2 ms, the amount the project already judged to be left near the round-trip floor.

## Six catch-ups where rivals or papers are already ahead

### 4. State-checkpoint prefix cache for recurrent models

**Idea.** Store exact recurrent-state checkpoints at chunk boundaries and at radix-tree branch points, resume from the deepest one and recompute the gap. **Gap.** The prefix cache is off for Qwen3.5 and 3.6 and state snapshots at block boundaries are listed as not done ([usage](../../docs/usage.md), [internals](../../docs/internals.md)); for the streamed 35B a cold 512-token prefill takes 4.85 / 4.77 s and moves 11.6 GB from the SSD ([RESULTS 1s](../RESULTS.md)), so every repeated prefix is paid for in SSD reads. This is the family the project markets. **Outside evidence.** Admitting at most two checkpoints per sequence at branch points gave 4.5-34.4x the token hit rate of server engines extended to hybrid models ([Marconi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19379)); a dynamic program can place a sparse set of exact checkpoints (abstract only) ([Sparse Prefix Caching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05219)); branching-point caching raised hit rate from 43.8% to 60.8% and cut mean time to first token from 1.57 s to 1.07 s (vendor-reported) ([SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)). Real coding-agent traces show a prefix-cache hit rate above 96% ([vLLM AgentX](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx)). llama.cpp still loses prompt reuse on hybrid models when slots are saved to disk ([llama.cpp digest, Aug 2026](https://buttondown.com/weekly-project-news/archive/weekly-github-report-for-llamacpp-august-04-2026-1387/)), and vllm-metal's block-boundary state mode is experimental ([vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0)). **Exact and agnostic.** Exact; applies to any model with recurrent state. **Expected gain (estimate).** A turn sharing a 512-token prefix on the streamed 35B saves up to about 4.8 s cold; on the in-memory 9B at about 220 tok/s prefill ([RESULTS 1zb](../RESULTS.md)) a 2,048-token shared prefix saves about 9 s. Cost is 31-63 MB per checkpoint by the plan's own estimate ([internals](../../docs/internals.md)). **Effort.** One to two weeks; per-slot states and recompute-on-preempt exist. **First experiment.** A two-turn chat on Qwen3.5-9B with a checkpoint at the last block boundary: confirm a token-identical continuation and time turn two against a full re-prefill.

### 5. Time the MLA decode kernel and switch it on

**Idea.** The dedicated kernel exists behind `MJ_MLA_DECODE=kernel`; its speed has never been recorded. **Gap.** DeepSeek-V2-Lite decodes at 61.00, 52.24, 33.82 and 22.17 tok/s at depth 0, 2k, 8k and 16k, and trails vllm-mlx at depth 0 (66.02) ([RESULTS 1zh, 1zm](../RESULTS.md)); the default path copies the layer's whole latent cache per token and runs unfused attention at width 576. **Outside evidence.** Exact merging of partial softmax results across context splits is the standard remedy ([LeanAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10480), self-reported and not independently replicated); a community MLX server reports 53.2 to 68.4 tok/s at 16k after a flash-decoding route on M5-class GPUs (self-reported) ([MTPLX releases](https://github.com/youssofal/MTPLX/releases)); the project's own split-K gave +11.8% at 8,192 on Qwen3.5-9B ([RESULTS 1x](../RESULTS.md)). **Exact and agnostic.** The kernel is closer to float32 than the current default (error 0.006-0.008 against 0.015-0.030), so tokens can differ at near-ties, toward the reference. MLA only. **Expected gain.** The prize is the context-dependent cost: 45.1 ms per token at 16k against 16.4 ms at depth 0, a difference of 28.7 ms, and 13.2 ms at 8k (reciprocals of the rates above; my arithmetic). How much the kernel recovers is unmeasured. **Effort.** Hours to measure; default switch gated by the long-context accuracy check (checklist 0.15). **First experiment.** Interleaved `sdpa` against `kernel` at depth 0, 2k, 8k and 16k.

### 7. Phase-aware streamed prefill

**Idea.** Treat prefill and decode as different residency regimes. A prefill chunk touches nearly every expert, so sweep each layer's expert file sequentially once per chunk and make the chunk as large as memory allows; then hand decode a slot set warmed with the prompt's hottest experts. **Gap.** A cold 512-token prompt moves 11.6 GB (5.8 GB warm) at 1.7-2.2 GB/s effective against a 3.2 GB/s ceiling ([RESULTS 1s](../RESULTS.md)); the project named expert-major prefill and prompt-hot experts as the next levers ([RESULTS 1d](../RESULTS.md)); the effect of prefill prefetch on the following decode is unsettled at one run each ([RESULTS 1s](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** With an LRU under a cyclic layer scan, baselines pull 86-99% of prefill expert traffic from SSD although RAM holds 44% of the pool; phase-aware layouts cut that to 52% and improve time to first token 2.40x on average (RTX 5090, capped RAM) ([Tideway](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.14848v1)). The share of experts touched grows from 47% at 32 tokens to 67% at 256 ([FlashMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17063v1)). Warming the cache from prefill frequencies gave 1.96x decode speed over an empty cache on a modelled device ([SliceMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12990v3)). **Exact and agnostic.** Exact, with the known caveat that slot settings change bf16 accumulation order at exact ties ([RESULTS 1s](../RESULTS.md)); applies to any streamed MoE. **Expected gain (estimate).** Reads moving from 1.7-2.2 to 3.2 GB/s is 1.5-1.9x on the SSD-bound part; larger chunks also cut the number of sweeps. Effect on decode is unknown. **Effort.** One to two weeks. **First experiment.** For real prompts of 512, 1,024 and 2,048 tokens, record bytes read and seconds under today's path and under one sequential sweep per chunk, then decode 128 tokens with and without a prompt-hot warm set, interleaved. Random prompts overstate expert coverage, so this must use real text.

### 9. Run-length-aware expert tiles in prefill

**Idea.** Pick the tile height of the expert matmul from the number of rows routed to each expert and align runs to tile boundaries. **Gap.** Experts are 4.31-4.35 s of a 6.1 s instrumented gpt-oss prefill and run at 2.81 TFLOPS at 512 tokens against 3.31-3.38 for dense quantized matmuls; pp512 is 1.06x behind llama.cpp (428.84 against 406-408) ([RESULTS 1zc, 1zk](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** A community pull request that chooses the tile by rows per expert reports +12-22% on the kernel, 96% of an equivalent dense quantized matmul, and +6.2% MoE prefill at 32k on an M3 Max, a chip without matrix units; its merge status is unknown ([mlx issue #3957](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/3957)). On M5 the waste is far larger: 20.8% of the ceiling at 16 rows per expert, and 11.94 against 6.85 TFLOPS for aligned against shifted runs ([mlx issue #3925](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/3925)). MLX 0.32.3 lists gather_qmm improvements by title only ([MLX releases](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/releases)), so part of this may already be in the project's MLX 0.32.4. Two sibling pull requests are worth the same check: one fuses the causal mask into the softmax of the unfused attention fallback (+2.8% prefill), the path the project uses for head sizes 192 and 256, and one removes per-token index rebuilding (+2.0-2.8% decode) ([mlx issue #3957](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/issues/3957)). **Exact and agnostic.** Same math; bit equality must be checked because the project forbids rounding changes. Applies to any MoE. **Expected gain (estimate).** If the expert matmul reached 96% of the dense rate, it would run 1.13-1.15x faster; experts are about 71% of instrumented prefill, so about +9-10% at 512-token chunks, more than the 1.06x gap. The instrumented split overstates synced parts, so treat this as an upper estimate. **What stays closed.** The grouped MXFP4 kernel and padded layouts at decode batch sizes ([RESULTS 5](../RESULTS.md)). **Effort.** Days for the micro-benchmark, up to two weeks for a kernel. **First experiment.** Sorted gather_qmm against the equivalent dense quantized matmul at 16, 32, 64 and 256 rows per expert on this M4 with MLX 0.32.4.

### 12. 8-bit KV with attention computed in the 8-bit domain

**Idea.** Store K and V in 8 bits and run both attention products on the 8-bit data, skipping layers with small windows. **Gap.** Planned item 3.3 with nothing measured; 32k is an open capacity check, and mlx-lm was left out at 16k on DeepSeek because expanded K/V needs about 4.5 GB more ([LAUNCH_TODO](../LAUNCH_TODO.md), [RESULTS 1zi](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** FP8 KV with FP8 attention halves cache bytes; the latency slope against context fell to 54% of bf16 on an 8B model with break-even near 7k tokens, and for gpt-oss-20b to 71% with sliding-window layers left in bf16; a 128k retrieval test scored 91% in bf16, 13% with the first FP8 kernel and 89% after two-level float32 accumulation ([vLLM FP8 KV](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)). Four-bit storage costs 10-68% latency when it must be dequantized, and 3-bit fails at 128k-256k ([vLLM TurboQuant study](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-05-11-turboquant)). Splash ships INT8 KV by default (vendor) ([Splash README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/incoai/splash/main/README.md)). **Exact and agnostic.** Changes outputs; model-agnostic. **Expected gain.** Twice the KV capacity. Speed gain is small at the project's contexts: Qwen3.5-9B pays about 1.07 ms per token more at depth 2,048 than at depth 0 ([RESULTS 1x](../RESULTS.md)), and the outside break-even is about 7k tokens. **Effort.** Two to three weeks, and it cannot ship before the long-context teacher-forced gate exists; the project already recorded the same lesson from another build, which matched on short traces and failed at 64k ([LAUNCH_TODO](../LAUNCH_TODO.md)). **First experiment.** A retrieval test at the longest supported context on the current bf16 path, to create the gate.

### 17. Quality-per-byte modes

**Idea.** Two opt-in modes that change outputs: bits allocated per expert by sensitivity and activation frequency, and an economy mode that serves a low-gate-weight miss from a lower-precision or resident substitute. **Gap.** llama.cpp's Q4_K_M is the better 4-bit file (KL 0.081 against 0.159 on OLMoE); at matched quality the project's OLMoE decode lead shrinks to about 12% and prefill falls to 84-91% of llama.cpp ([RESULTS 1ze](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** Per-expert mixed precision chosen by an integer program gives up to 25% more throughput than uniform 4-bit and perplexity 7.01 against 8.49 at 2.25 bits on DeepSeek-V2-Lite ([MxMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05799v1)); attention needs more bits than expert layers and shared experts more than routed ones (snippet only) ([QuantMoE-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08155v2)). Loading a low-precision copy on a miss when the gate weight is small stayed within 1% accuracy on Mixtral ([HOBBIT](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01433v2)). The risk is model-specific: executing substituted experts barely moved gpt-oss-20b (0.779 against 0.799) and collapsed Qwen3-30B-A3B (0.817 to 0.641) ([Speculating Experts](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19289v1)), and cache-biased routing was validated at 7-21% residual miss rates and reported to fail below 5%, the regime an SSD-backed laptop needs ([Cache-Conditional Experts](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00099v2), [SliceMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12990v3)). **Exact and agnostic.** Neither exact nor free of per-model calibration. **Expected gain.** Not estimated; the reported figures come from other hardware and other models. **What stays closed.** Rotation-based 3-bit experts and entropy coding of 4-bit codes ([RESULTS 1s](../RESULTS.md)). **Effort.** Weeks, plus the quality-matched comparisons in checklist 0.3. **First experiment.** Measure gate-weight distribution on misses for gpt-oss-20b and Qwen3.6-35B; if low-weight misses are rare, the economy mode has nothing to work with.

## Hardware levers: one is blocked on a machine, the rest start with cheap probes

### 10. M5 matrix units through Metal 4 tensor ops

**Idea.** M5-family GPUs carry a matrix multiply-accumulate unit per core, reachable only through Metal 4 tensor ops; move the project's multi-row kernels (prefill experts, fused glue, verify rows) onto that path and keep single-row decode on the current kernels. **Gap.** No result exists beyond one M4 (checklist 4.1); prefill here is compute-bound at 2.8-3.7 TFLOPS ([RESULTS 1zc](../RESULTS.md)). The project's custom kernels are built from simdgroup pieces and will not use the matrix units, so on an M5 MLX's own kernels could overtake them in multi-row regimes. **Outside evidence.** Apple reports 3.3-4.1x time to first token and 1.19-1.27x generation for M5 against M4 with MLX, including gpt-oss-20b at 3.33x ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/exploring-llms-mlx-m5)). A native Metal runtime reports prefill up to 3.9x MLX on M5 Pro and 2,788 against 2,426 tok/s at pp2048 on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, and deliberately keeps decode off the tensor path because decode is bandwidth-bound ([BaseRT M5](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19438)). A row of MXFP4 weights can be rewritten as one INT8 vector times a power-of-two scale for 97.9-99.97% of rows, giving 1.10-1.57x prefill over MLX on two MoE models (abstract only) ([Integer-Exact Microscaling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00486)); gpt-oss experts are MXFP4, so this bears directly on its prefill. OS 27 adds 4-bit and 8-bit float types with block-scale planes ([WWDC26 session 330](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/330/)). On chips without the units the same source runs on ordinary shader cores with no gain ([Rigel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12765), [llama.cpp PR #16634](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/16634)), and one engine found the tensor matmul 8% slower for single-row decode ([flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)). **Exact and agnostic.** Model-agnostic on M5 and later. Outputs need re-verification: engine authors report that the tensor matmul mishandles bfloat cooperative tensors ([llama.cpp PR #16634](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/16634)). **Expected gain.** Not estimable without hardware. **Effort.** Access to an M5 Mac, then weeks. **First experiment.** Run the unchanged suite on any M5 and compare each custom kernel with MLX's built-in at the same shape; the verify-row cost table from direction 3 is the most interesting row, because matrix units sit idle during decode in every engine.

### 13. Paced decode and a Low Power Mode energy probe

**Idea.** If decode is bound by memory bandwidth, a lower GPU clock should cut power more than speed. Low Power Mode is the only documented user control over GPU frequency. **Gap.** Energy on this GPU follows arithmetic rather than bytes ([RESULTS 1l](../RESULTS.md)); the energy cost of overlap is unresolved and the keep-alive's is unmeasured ([RESULTS 1h, 1u](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** On an M4 Pro, Low Power Mode caps GPU frequency at 67%, cuts GPU power from about 20 W to about 6 W and keeps 65-71% of performance on a compute-bound workload ([Eclectic Light](https://eclecticlight.co/2025/01/06/power-modes-and-apple-silicon-gpus/)), which is about 2.2x performance per watt (0.69 / 0.32, my arithmetic). On servers the decode phase is comparatively insensitive to frequency and separate decode frequency control saved up to 34% energy ([DVFS characterization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08219), [GreenLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16449v1)). No source tests this on a laptop SoC, and the project's own data cuts the other way at deep clock drops: the same matvec takes 1,543 µs after 5 ms of idle against 366 µs ([RESULTS 1u](../RESULTS.md)). **Exact and agnostic.** Yes to both. **Expected gain.** Unknown; that is why it is a probe. **Effort.** Hours. **First experiment.** Interleaved Qwen3.5-9B decode in normal and Low Power Mode, recording tok/s, GPU rail and whole-machine energy. No published benchmark states its power mode, so the result is publishable either way.

### 14. SME2 co-execution in prefill

**Idea.** The M4's performance cluster has a matrix unit worth about 2 TFLOPS in FP32 that no shipping engine uses beside the GPU; give it a fixed share of prefill rows. **Gap.** Small-model prefill trails by 1-5% (OLMoE 0.95x of the best rival; Qwen3.5-9B pp512 217.47 against 227.01) ([RESULTS 1zh, 1zb](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** Co-running lifts prefill from 232 to 304.5 tok/s at 1,024 tokens (1.31x) and does nothing for decode, where CPU plus GPU read 95.4 GB/s against 96.7 for the GPU alone ([EdgeAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.03394)); a per-layer row split improves time to first token 1.18-1.25x through stock MLX-LM, and inside MLX's lazy graph the split runs at 0.66x unless an eager boundary is inserted ([FusionML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22785)). Apple's own library reaches 19.0 GFLOPS at batch 1 against 85.0 for a custom kernel ([EdgeAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.03394)). All tests used dense models; neither paper reports energy. **Exact and agnostic.** Not token-identical by default: one paper reports identical output in 5 of 6 configurations, and llama.cpp's CPU and GPU paths agree on only 96.5-97.8% of next tokens ([FusionML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22785), [Splash README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/incoai/splash/main/README.md)). **Expected gain.** At best the reported 1.18-1.31x on in-memory models; nothing for gpt-oss on 16 GB, where prefill is memory-limited. The CPU's energy is invisible to the GPU rail, so this cannot be judged before direction 2. **Effort.** Weeks; no 4-bit SME kernel is described in the notes. **First experiment.** Time one dense quantized layer at prefill shape on the SME unit alone, and measure the GPU's slowdown while it runs.

### 15. Neural Engine spike

**Idea.** Run the plan's one-day spike (item 5.1): a dense fixed-shape block at prefill chunk sizes on the Neural Engine, judged on whole-machine energy. **Gap.** Phase 5 is open and no Neural Engine energy number exists ([LAUNCH_TODO](../LAUNCH_TODO.md)). **Outside evidence.** The measured M4 Neural Engine peaks at 19 TFLOPS FP16 (5.7 for a plain 2,048-square matmul) at 6.6 TFLOPS per watt against about 1.0 for the M4 GPU, with about 32 MB of on-chip memory ([maderix](https://maderix.substack.com/p/inside-the-m4-apple-neural-engine-615)); it streams 64 GB/s on an M4, and 152 GB/s on an M6 ([ANEMLL-bench](https://github.com/Anemll/anemll-bench)). For MoE prefill, one attention-plus-MoE block took 0.99 s and 54.72 J on the GPU against 1.26 s and 12.21 J on the Neural Engine, but with static capacity tiers and token dropping, so not exact ([NPUMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18788)). A GPU-plus-Neural-Engine prefill split improved cold time to first token 1.14x on an M5 Max for a dense 27B (snippet only) ([Splash 1.3.0 coverage](https://alphasignal.ai/news/inco-ai-s-splash-1-3-0-cuts-mac-prefill-wait-by-1-49x-for-coding-agents)). **A correction to the notes.** One researcher wrote that "Anemll-Forge" does not exist as such. It does: the repository ([Anemll/anemll-forge](https://github.com/Anemll/anemll-forge), branch v8-gpu-ane-prefill) and its Hugging Face article were read directly earlier in this session. It is a hybrid Neural Engine plus GPU research build for a dense Qwen3.8-27B on M6 that reads the Neural Engine's compiled weights in place, which removes the second-copy objection in the project's own Neural Engine note, and it states that it needs System Integrity Protection turned off. Its comparison against Splash 1.7.0 is one run per point, not interleaved, so it is not evidence of a lead. **Exact and agnostic.** Not exact (FP16 on a different device); applies only to dense fixed-shape parts. **Expected gain.** Energy, not speed; no estimate for this project. **Effort.** One day for the spike. **First experiment.** As planned in 5.1, and only after direction 2 can measure it.

### 16. SSD read path and read-energy accounting

**Idea.** Sweep reader threads and read sizes for 1-2 MB expert reads, test whether SSD traffic slows the GPU, and put a number on read energy. **Gap.** The SSD ceiling is 3.17-3.3 GB/s and one 13.2 MB expert costs 4.2 ms whatever the chunking ([RESULTS 1d.3](../RESULTS.md)); the GPU rail does not see reads or reader threads ([RESULTS 1d](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** Sparse flash reads need many threads (32 in Apple's own benchmarks) ([LLM in a Flash](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.11514v3)); no published sweep exists for Apple internal SSDs in this read range. One engine measured background SSD transfers slowing the GPU by 73% on unified memory with 6.75 MB experts ([flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)). Flash reads cost about 102 pJ per bit, roughly 25x fast DRAM, and prefetching hides latency but not energy (analytical study, data-center parts) ([SSD offloading considered harmful](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06978)). **Exact and agnostic.** Yes to both. **Expected gain (estimate).** Small for decode speed. For energy, the project's 0.116 GB of SSD bytes per decode step at 96 slots (an earlier state, [RESULTS 1d.3](../RESULTS.md)) at 102 pJ per bit is roughly 0.1 J per token, about 40% of the 0.220-0.243 J GPU-rail figure recorded for the same model. The per-bit figure is not for Apple's SSD, so this is order-of-magnitude only; it is still large enough that a GPU-rail energy claim for a streamed model would mislead. **Effort.** Hours to days. **First experiment.** GPU step time for a fixed in-memory kernel loop with and without concurrent reads at the streamer's read rate.

## Measurement leads: nobody publishes joules per token

### 2. Whole-machine energy per token with interleaved rounds

**Idea.** Report energy above idle for the whole machine beside the GPU rail, in interleaved rounds against each rival, with an idle baseline, a temperature gate, and power mode, mains state, context length and phase split stated. **Gap.** Every published number is the IOReport GPU rail; whole-machine energy is planned (checklist 0.16) and absent from RESULTS; CPU and DRAM rails returned impossible deltas in 7 of 207 rows ([RESULTS 1w, 1zb](../RESULTS.md), [LAUNCH_TODO](../LAUNCH_TODO.md)). The gated and streamed paths spin a CPU core, which the GPU rail does not see, and run a GPU keep-alive whose energy cost is recorded as not yet measured ([RESULTS 1u, 1v](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence.** Energy per token is published by none of llama.cpp, mlx-lm, LM Studio, Ollama, vllm-mlx, vllm-metal, oMLX, Splash, uzu or BaseRT; no validation of Apple's modelled rails against a wall meter exists; a community repository retired its figures after finding the battery gauge moves in 5% steps (snippet only) ([apple-silicon-llm-bench](https://github.com/john-rocky/apple-silicon-llm-bench)); sources disagree on whether Apple's estimates include DRAM ([Intelligence-per-Watt](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/intelligence-per-watt/benchmarking/platform-support/)); tokens per watt halves each time context doubles in one analytical model, so a figure without context length is meaningless ([tokens-per-watt paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17280v1)). **Expected gain.** No speed. It turns the 16-28% GPU-rail energy lead on gpt-oss ([RESULTS 1zi, 1zk](../RESULTS.md)) into a defensible claim or corrects it, and it is the precondition for judging directions 1, 13, 14, 15 and 16. **Effort.** Days. **First experiment.** gpt-oss-20b decode, ours against LM Studio, interleaved, GPU rail and whole-machine above idle side by side; then the same for one streamed model.

### 6. Real prompts and a fidelity column

**Idea.** Replace random-token prompts in the standard grid with fixed real-text sets, report speculative rows separately, and publish next-token agreement with the float32 reference for every engine. **Gap.** Streamed decode ranges from 19.76 to 32.20 tok/s by seed ([RESULTS 1zb](../RESULTS.md)); the real-chat check, the long-context check and the benchmark subsets are not run (checklist 0.12, 0.15, 0.2). **Outside evidence.** Random-token prompts overstate speculative throughput by about 23% and distort MoE routing ([SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)); a text-calibrated expert set hit 42% against 6% for random profiles ([llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324)); only three of nine Mac stacks pass completion, fidelity and coverage gates ([SiliconBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19169)); Splash publishes 97.8-99.5% agreement with llama.cpp, which is not exactness ([Splash README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/incoai/splash/main/README.md)). **Expected gain.** No speed; it decides whether the streamed multiples survive real text, in either direction. **Effort.** Days. **First experiment.** Re-run the Qwen3.6-35B and Qwen3-30B decode rows on three real prompts, interleaved with llama.cpp.

### 11. Attribution traces and three variance tests

**Idea.** Record one per-part trace each for OLMoE, DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Qwen3-30B, which have none, and test three outside hypotheses for unexplained numbers. **Gap.** Depth-0 losses of 1% and 8% to vllm-mlx are unattributed ([RESULTS 1zh](../RESULTS.md)); the exact gpt-oss path gave 33.16-33.99 and later 27.86-30.13 tok/s for the same command ([RESULTS 1za, 1zc](../RESULTS.md)); a 14 MB projection ran at 24-34 GB/s in a streamed trace against about 90 GB/s elsewhere ([RESULTS 1t](../RESULTS.md)). **Outside evidence and hypotheses.** First, MLX commits a command buffer after 40 operations or 40 MB on base and Pro chips and blocks at 10 in-flight buffers ([MLX device.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx/main/mlx/backend/metal/device.cpp), [MLX scheduler.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ml-explore/mlx/main/mlx/scheduler.cpp)); the notes record the project raising the megabyte limit but say nothing about the operation limit, and at 40 operations per buffer a step of 2,088 operations (the 9B's count, [RESULTS 1i](../RESULTS.md)) would split into about 52 buffers (my division). Whether the project already swept this limit is not stated in the notes. Second, a saturating background memory workload slows batch-1 decode by 72% on a base M4, and demoting it to efficiency cores makes it worse by 13.9 points ([The Gap Is the Loop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/170048)); this fits a state that is neither thermal nor kernel-level. Third, SSD transfers competing for the memory controller ([flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)) is an alternative to the clock-drop explanation for the slow streamed projection. **Expected gain.** Up to the size of those gaps. **Effort.** Hours to days each. **First experiment.** Sweep `MLX_MAX_OPS_PER_BUFFER` on OLMoE depth-0 decode, interleaved.

## What the literature says not to do

The loudest warning is about speculative decoding. A NeurIPS 2026 workshop study found every method it evaluated lowered single-request throughput on four Apple machines (abstract only; per-method numbers were not retrievable) ([applicability study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31479)); an SSD-streamed engine found MTP break-even "because MoE I/O scales per token" ([flash-moe](https://github.com/Anemll/flash-moe)); a separate-draft-model study on MLX reached 1.7x only on structured text because both models are bandwidth-bound (abstract only) ([Bielik](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16368)); and MoE verification overhead of 2-3x is established on servers ([Cascade](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20675v1)). The project's results are both an exception and a confirmation. The exception: 2.0-2.1x on the dense 9B with the model's own head at depth 2, token-identical ([RESULTS 1k](../RESULTS.md)), which works because the head shares the trunk and the exact small-row kernels keep 2-3 verify rows nearly free in time. The confirmation: on MoE the gain falls to about 1.15-1.2x on gpt-oss and +7-14% on the streamed 35B, for the reason the papers give, and disappears at eight requests ([RESULTS 1p, 1u, 1r](../RESULTS.md)). The project adds a finding the literature lacks: rejected rows cost energy even when time is flat ([RESULTS 1l](../RESULTS.md)). The outside evidence itself conflicts, since vendors report large gains from trained drafters on M5 ([Splash README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/incoai/splash/main/README.md)) and independent M1 Max tests show gains on short and code prompts that fade at 27k context ([lilting.ch](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/m1-max-llm-engine-benchmark)). The defensible reading is narrow: do not run deep drafts, separate draft models, or ungated speculation on MoE at batch 1; do run a native head at depth 1-2 behind a utility gate.

Several of the project's own rejections are independently corroborated, which raises confidence that they should stay closed. Handing memory-mapped experts to Metal wires the whole view: one engine saw wired memory go from 3.4 to 65.2 GiB, another measured a 5x slowdown ([ds4 issue #638](https://github.com/antirez/ds4/issues/638), [flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)), matching [RESULTS 1p, 1y](../RESULTS.md). Predicting from the previous token's experts hit 25% and cost 18% ([flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)), consistent with adjacent tokens sharing about a quarter of a layer's experts on Qwen3.6-35B ([Edge0](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18063v2)). Read-ahead hints netted zero, co-activation file layouts gave nothing, compressing the expert cache lost 13%, and spin-polling the GPU lost 23% ([flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)). Segmented LRU gave no gain over LRU ([llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324)). Staging in threadgroup memory is unnecessary for peak matmul rate ([BaseRT M5](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19438)), in line with [RESULTS 1l](../RESULTS.md).

Beyond those: CPU plus GPU co-execution does nothing for decode ([EdgeAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.03394)); 3-bit KV fails at 128k and beyond, and query-aware KV eviction breaks on multi-turn reuse ([vLLM TurboQuant study](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-05-11-turboquant), [KVzip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416)); lazily backed Metal buffers do not save physical memory ([demand-paging post](https://rockyrunnr.github.io/posts/demand-paging-fails-apple-silicon-gpu)); Core ML dispatch of 20-24 ms rules out the Neural Engine at layer granularity ([FusionML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22785)); and lowering the priority of background jobs during a benchmark hurts decode more than leaving them on performance cores ([The Gap Is the Loop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/170048)).

## Where the notes disagree or run thin

Overlapping SSD reads with GPU work is contested. One engine measured a 38% loss from speculative early routing and attributes it to SSD transfers sharing the memory controller ([flash-moe](https://github.com/danveloper/flash-moe)); others report gains from reading during compute ([Edge0](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.18063v2), [AiiStream](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/running-qwen3-6-35b-a3b-on-a-8-16-gb-mac-at-15-token-s/182775)), and the project's own depth-2 prefetch is worth +4-8% ([RESULTS 1s](../RESULTS.md)). Expert size is the likely variable (6.75 MB experts in the negative case, about 1.8 MB in the positive ones), but nobody has tested that. Popularity skew is also disputed: a tester found near-uniform routing on DeepSeek-V4-Flash and the author of the change disagreed ([llama.cpp PR #26563](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/26563)), so no cache policy should be assumed to transfer across families.

Several figures conflict outright. Neural Engine call overhead is 0.095 ms through a private interface ([maderix](https://maderix.substack.com/p/inside-the-m4-apple-neural-engine-615)), about 0.2 ms as the project's own note records it ([neural-engine](../../docs/neural-engine.md)), and 20-24 ms through Core ML ([FusionML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22785)); these are different access paths, not one number. On System Integrity Protection, the researcher's sources run the private interface from a normal process, while Anemll-Forge states it needs protection off; both can be true for different mechanisms. Apple's two talks disagree on whether cooperative tensors as matmul inputs arrived in OS 26.3 or OS 27 ([tech talk 111432](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/tech-talks/111432/), [WWDC26 session 330](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/330/)). A review puts the M4 MacBook Pro's SSD at about 2.0 GB/s ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/laptops/macbooks/m5-macbook-pros-ssd-is-2-5x-faster-on-average-than-last-gen-m4-exceeding-apples-own-claims-m5-achieves-6-000-mb-s-across-both-read-and-write-speeds)) while the project measured 3.17-3.3 GB/s on its own ([RESULTS 1d.3](../RESULTS.md)); cross-machine streaming comparisons are unsafe until one tool measures both. The integer-exact MXFP4 format is reported as more accurate than MLX's MXFP4 and, in a search summary of the same paper, less accurate than llama.cpp's types on MoE ([Integer-Exact Microscaling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00486)). A custom gather kernel is reported at 1.03-1.12x end to end over MLX's (snippet only) ([applicability study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31479)), against the project's finding that its grouped kernel was slower ([RESULTS 5](../RESULTS.md)); the outside figure is too thin to reopen that.

One outside measurement independently confirms a project constant: the M4 GPU streams up to 96.7 GB/s of a nominal 120 ([EdgeAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.03394)), against the project's 94-99 GB/s ([RESULTS 1i](../RESULTS.md)). Another matches a project gain: pipelining the token loop is worth 9-12% on a base M4 ([The Gap Is the Loop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/170048)), against 1.14-1.15x for overlap ([RESULTS 1g](../RESULTS.md)).

Inside the project's own documents, the Neural Engine note says whole-machine energy is reported from RESULTS 1zm onward, and RESULTS 1zm contains none; the internals note still lists overlap scheduling as a gap although it exists. Most rejections rest on one or two paired rounds on a noisy 16 GB laptop, some taken during a large download, so each means "no gain at this budget on this machine". The internal review read docstrings, not code, and a benchmark was running while it was written.

The thinnest evidence under the top-ranked ideas: no multi-layer prediction accuracy for current many-expert models; no standalone recall for Edge0's next-token predictor; no energy measurement for any prefetch, cache or speculative method on a real memory-bound device; and no M5 measurement of anything in this project.

## Conclusion

The project's most valuable assets are measurements nobody else has published: the GPU's clock drop within milliseconds of idling, the cost of a shared-event round trip, the wired-memory ceiling and its kill behaviour, and energy that tracks arithmetic rather than bytes. The 2026 literature is rich in predictors and cache policies evaluated on desktop GPUs with experts in host RAM, and almost empty on exact operation, unified memory, SSD lead time and energy. That is where a named idea can land: expert virtual memory whose pager runs on a forecast and whose indirection stays on the GPU timeline, with speculation priced in joules above it. The offline oracle on the existing 8,192-token trace is the single experiment that decides whether the top idea is worth three weeks, and it needs no GPU.

The ordering of work that follows from this is not the ranking itself. The energy protocol and the real-prompt grid come first because they are cheap and every later claim depends on them; the trace oracle and the MLA timing run alongside because they cost hours; the scheduler work for speculation and the recurrent prefix cache are the long poles that turn existing results into product. The largest strategic exposure is hardware: every rival's headline number is now an M5 number, and the project's custom kernels bypass the one new accelerator those machines have.
