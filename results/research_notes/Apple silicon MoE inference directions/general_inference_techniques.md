# General (model-agnostic) LLM inference-engine techniques, state of the art as of November 2026

Scope: system-level and kernel-level techniques a single-device engine could adopt, for one user or a few concurrent requests on a memory-bandwidth-bound unified-memory device, 16k-128k contexts, energy per token as a first-class metric.

Reading notes for the report writer:
- "Background" marks pre-2025 work. Everything else is 2025-2026.
- "Vendor" marks numbers reported by the project that ships the feature (not independent).
- "Abstract only" marks papers where only the abstract was read; details beyond it are not verified.
- Almost all measured numbers are from NVIDIA server GPUs. Numbers from Apple silicon or phones are called out explicitly, because they are the ones that transfer.
- Retraining column meaning: "none" = works on the released weights; "calibration" = needs a small offline pass over data but no gradient training of the base model; "aux training" = a separate small draft/head must be trained (base model frozen); "native" = the model vendor ships the piece.

## 1. Speculative decoding as of 2026

### Takeaway
The field moved from sequential feature-level drafters (EAGLE-3) to parallel drafters (P-EAGLE, DFlash, DSpark) and vendor-shipped MTP heads, and the newest engine work is not the drafter but the cost model: choosing how many draft rows to verify per step from measured step costs and per-position acceptance estimates. Reported acceptance is strongly domain- and context-length-dependent (coding high, roleplay low, drafters degrade past their training context), synthetic prompts overstate gains by about 23%, and on bandwidth-bound hardware short drafts (depth 1-3) are repeatedly the best choice.

### Cited Findings

Drafter families and their gains
- EAGLE-3 (March 2025): feature-level drafter with "training-time test", draft tree from EAGLE-2; reports speedup up to 6.5x and about 1.4x over EAGLE-2. Retraining: aux training (drafter only). — [EAGLE-3 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)
- EAGLE 3.1 (May 26, 2026; EAGLE team + vLLM + TorchSpec): adds FC normalization after each target hidden state and feeds post-norm hidden states into the next draft step. Motivation is "attention drift": with deeper speculation the drafter attends more to its own tokens, and EAGLE-3 degraded with different chat templates, long contexts and out-of-distribution system prompts. Claims up to 2x longer acceptance length on long-context workloads (no context lengths given). Vendor data point: Kimi K2.6 draft, SPEED-Bench coding, 2.03x per-user throughput at concurrency 1, 1.71x at 4, 1.66x at 16. Existing EAGLE-3 checkpoints stay compatible. No real-traffic numbers. — [vLLM blog: EAGLE 3.1](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-05-26-eagle-3-1)
- Parallel drafting taxonomy (July 28, 2026): P-EAGLE maps verifier hidden states to several future positions in one step; DFlash projects verifier hidden states into the speculator's KV cache and generates a block by block diffusion; DSpark is the DFlash backbone plus a light autoregressive correction head and a confidence head. Parallel drafting makes draft latency independent of draft length. The post gives no numeric acceptance; its Figure 1 numbers were corrected in an erratum on July 29, 2026. — [vLLM blog: parallel drafting](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-07-28-speculators-parallel-drafting)
- DFlash (ICML 2026): block-diffusion drafter conditioned on target features, drafts in a single forward pass; abstract claims "over 6x lossless acceleration" and "up to 2.5x higher speedup" than EAGLE-3 (ratio of speedups). Abstract only. Retraining: aux training. — [DFlash paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06036)
- DFlash on an Apple laptop: ExecuTorch running a 30B model on an M5 Pro (64 GiB) goes from 21.6 tok/s to 33.0 tok/s with DFlash (+52.8%); target and draft share weights and are exported in one artifact; block length is selectable at run time; both greedy and rejection sampling are supported. Vendor. — [PyTorch blog, Aug 2026](https://pytorch.org/blog/fast-ondevice-agentic-ai-with-executorch/)
- SGLang DFlash2 (v0.5.19, Sep 2026): local convolution and candidate selector, 3.43x over no speculation at batch 1. Vendor. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- SpecForge (March 2026): open training framework for EAGLE-3 drafters (training tooling, not a new decode algorithm). — [SpecForge paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18567)

MTP heads shipped with models
- llama.cpp MTP support (PR #22673, opened May 4, merged May 16, 2026): MTP head loads from the same GGUF as a separate model with its own context and KV cache ("a single layer transformer + kv cache"); a per-ubatch hook passes hidden features to it; `--spec-type draft-mtp`, depth via `--spec-draft-n-max`. Tested on Qwen3.6 27B and 35B-A3B. Retraining: native. — [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673)
- Numbers from that PR thread: RTX 3090+3060, 27B Q6_K, 22.97 -> 42.45 tok/s (about 1.85x) at 76% acceptance; RTX 5090 q4_0, 64 -> 105 tok/s at 0.80 acceptance; DGX Spark 27B Q8, acceptance 0.83 at depth 2 vs 0.72 at depth 3; 6 GB laptop GPU, 35B-A3B: baseline 22.9, depth 2 gives 29.4 tok/s (acceptance 0.82), depth 4 gives 26.1 tok/s (acceptance 0.64). Costs: prefill dropped from about 1315 to 665 tok/s on one setup (hidden-state device-to-host transfers), about 2.5 GiB extra memory on one setup, requires `n_parallel=1`. Metal was tested but no numbers were posted. — [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673)
- Independent single-machine tests: RTX 3090, Qwen3.6 27B, 38 -> 65 tok/s (1.71x) — [DataCamp tutorial](https://datacamp.com/tutorial/multi-token-prediction-llama-cpp); on a 16 GB RTX 4080 MTP is a clear win for the 27B at IQ3_XXS but a net negative for the 35B in practical use because usable context shrinks — [dev.to test](https://dev.to/rosgluk/qwen-36-27b-and-35b-mtp-vs-standard-on-16gb-gpu-42jd)
- MTP performance-engineering study (arXiv 2609.35188, single request, NVIDIA A10G, 360 requests over plain text, reasoning, tool calling; two-token MTP): throughput 1.91x-2.19x, time to first output 10.0%-14.2% lower, median mean acceptance length 2.370-2.595 tokens per verification, 56.4%-78.1% fewer executions of the repeating CUDA graph per token. Key kernel fact: the dominant MTP GEMM kernel was not faster than the dominant autoregressive GEMV kernel, and in selected cases both approached the A10G memory-bandwidth limit; the gain is amortization (fewer repeated executions per token). Abstract only. — [Beneath the Tokens](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35188)
- LiteRT-LM (May 19, 2026): Gemma 4 MTP drafter runs on the same accelerator as the main model and shares KV cache and activations to avoid cross-hardware sync; up to 2.2x decode on a Samsung S26 Ultra GPU. Vendor. — [Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/blazing-fast-on-device-genai-with-litert-lm/)
- vllm-metal on Apple silicon (Sep 22, 2026): batched MTP on Gemma 4 E4B gives +20% output tok/s at concurrency 1, +0% at 8, +9% at 16; MTP there requires greedy sampling and `--no-async-scheduling`, and cannot yet be combined with the hybrid-model prefix cache. Vendor. — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0)
- MTP on MLX (community server, Sep-Nov 2026 releases, mostly M5 Max 128 GB): depth is per model (depth 1 default for one 27B model, where depth 3 was 14% slower; depth 2 for others); one head accepts 78.5% at position 1 and 56.5% at position 2; another about 75%; acceptance about 0.875 at depth 1 on a 9k prompt; a "block verification" acceptance rule was made distribution-exact for depths 1-4; draft heads are themselves quantized (8-bit; one 0.24 GB head). Decode at long context after kernel work: 68.4 tok/s at 16k, 60.9 at 100k, 44.2 at 206k for one MoE model. Self-reported by the project. — [MTPLX releases](https://github.com/youssofal/MTPLX/releases)
- A community pull request to mlx-lm (#990, opened March 13, 2026) proposes native MTP for Qwen3.5/3.6 with an `--mtp` flag, using MTP for single requests and the batch generator for concurrent ones; merge status not confirmed. — [mlx-lm PR #990](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-lm/pull/990)

Training-free / retrieval drafting (prompt-lookup, n-gram, suffix)
- SuffixDecoding (NeurIPS 2025 spotlight): suffix trees over prompts and previous outputs, no draft model or training; aimed at repetitive agentic requests; abstract reports up to 5.3x, 2.8x faster than EAGLE-2/3 and 1.9x faster than model-free baselines on those workloads; CMU write-up reports 1.8-4.5x over vanilla and 1.3-3x over prompt-lookup decoding. Shipped in vLLM through Snowflake's ArcticInference. Retraining: none. — [SuffixDecoding paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04975); [CMU blog](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~csd-phd-blog/2025/suffix-decoding/)
- Counter-evidence for plain n-gram drafting on general text: SPEED-Bench measured n-gram drafting on Llama 3.3 70B at mean acceptance length 1.41 and 0.88x (a slowdown) at batch 32, draft length 3. — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)
- llama.cpp treats MTP plus n-gram combination as experimental and "suitable for non-CUDA systems". — [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673)

Self-speculative / layer-skip
- Background: LayerSkip (Meta, 2024) trains with layer dropout and early-exit loss, drafts with early layers and verifies with the rest, sharing compute and activations; needs a specially trained model. — [LayerSkip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16710)
- DEL (COLM 2025): plug-in that picks exit layer and speculation length at run time from per-layer acceptance tracking. — [DEL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05598)
- An ICML 2026 characterization paper splits self-speculation into layer skipping vs early exit and notes no earlier benchmark isolated these methods. — [Self-speculative decoding characterization, ICML 2026](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~ganesa10/assets/pdfs/selfspecdecode-icml2026.pdf)

Vocabulary-pruned draft heads (two-stage head for the drafter)
- FR-Spec (ACL 2025): restrict the draft LM head to a frequency-ranked vocabulary subset, verify on the full vocabulary; cuts LM-head compute by 75%, average 1.12x over EAGLE-2; output distribution unchanged; no retraining. — [FR-Spec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14856)
- Cost of that idea: EAGLE-3's pruned vocabulary (usually 32k) misses about 22% of target tokens in SPEED-Bench's multilingual category; biggest accuracy drops in multilingual, RAG, summarization. — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)

Acceptance on realistic vs synthetic traffic
- SPEED-Bench (ICML 2026; vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang on B200): Llama 3.3 70B at draft length 3, temp 0: vanilla draft-model SD acceptance length 2.44 / 1.60x; EAGLE-3 2.44 / 1.90x; Qwen3-Next native MTP 2.81 / 1.20x. EAGLE-3 coding 3.00 vs roleplay 2.04. Random-token prompts overestimate SD throughput by about 23% on average and also distort MoE expert routing. Public EAGLE-3 drafters degrade at long input lengths; drafters trained at 1k-4k lose accuracy once the input exceeds the training length; YaRN scaling at inference recovers much of it. Native MTP holds up better than post-trained EAGLE-3 at longer draft lengths. — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)
- Acceptance by dataset for one production pair (DeepSeek-V4-Flash + DSpark, temp 1.0): GSM8K 0.781, HumanEval 0.751, MT-Bench 0.714, SPEED-Bench high-entropy 0.642. — [vLLM blog: adaptive verification, Oct 2026](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-14-adaptive-verification-online-estimator)
- Per-position acceptance falls from over 70% for the first drafted token to under 10% for the seventh on one model. — [vLLM blog: DSpark adaptive verification, Aug 2026](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-08-14-dspark-adaptive-verification)
- Agent traces (TraceSpec, arXiv 2610.41177; 10,000 train / 2,500 test states, browsing and software engineering): aggregate acceptance can rise with context length while falling within matched late browsing actions; training the drafter on intermediate assistant turns improves replay throughput 12.5%-22.6% over a DSpark base. Abstract only; a search summary of the paper adds that software-engineering acceptance peaks at 8k-16k and declines after, and that throughput falls at long contexts in both workloads. — [TraceSpec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41177)
- Real agentic coding traces (AgentX): median 43 turns per session, median input 142K tokens, median output 444 tokens, prefix-cache hit rate above 96%. — [vLLM blog: AgentX](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx)

Cost model for draft length / verify rows
- Speedup can be projected as (autoregressive step time x acceptance length) / speculative step time; projection 2.22x vs measured 2.23x. Optimal draft length depends on batch: longer drafts help when memory-bound at low batch; at higher batch, draft length 1 is preferred. — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)
- Confidence-scheduled verification (vLLM, Aug 2026): per-position confidences are multiplied into survival probabilities; a global top-B over all requests picks which draft slots get verified; B maximizes expected tokens per unit step time using cost tables profiled at startup (median of five dummy runs per shape, forced monotonic); verify cost is a staircase because of graph padding. — [vLLM blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-08-14-dspark-adaptive-verification)
- Online estimator without a confidence head (vLLM, Oct 2026): logistic regression on logit(q_max) with one shared slope and one intercept per draft position (K+1 parameters), refit online by IRLS using only draft tokens up to the first rejection; calibration ECE 0.0100 vs 0.0736 for raw q_max; features that depend on the sampled token were rejected because they would break the lossless guarantee. A smaller fixed length sometimes still beats adaptive at maximum length. — [vLLM blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-14-adaptive-verification-online-estimator)
- SGLang exposes adaptive speculative decoding with custom policies (v0.5.20) and DSpark sizing each verify window from draft confidence (v0.5.16; 383.7 tok/s at accept length about 5, batch 1). Vendor. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Unified-memory evidence: on Apple silicon with MLX-LM and a separate draft model (11B target, 1-1.5B drafts, k in {2,4,6}), speedup reaches 1.7x for structured text but fails for varied instructions; the stated reason is that both models are memory-bandwidth bound so sequential drafting is expensive relative to batched verification; the paper proposes a hardware-aware speedup formula. Abstract only. — [Bielik speculative decoding on Apple Silicon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16368)
- Flash-resident target models on phones (Lever, May 2026): draft model stays in DRAM, token tree built with an I/O- and compute-aware gain-cost objective, early-exit prediction prunes branches; 2.93x lower latency than flash-offloaded baseline and 1.50x vs conventional SD. Abstract only. — [Lever](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16786)

Lossless verification details
- Rejection sampling keeps SD lossless (output distribution matches the target). — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)
- SGLang v0.5.23 adds seeded rejection sampling for deterministic inference with drafts; v0.5.22 adds opt-in block verification (extended to the DFlash family) and fixes a bug where hybrid linear models resumed from wrong recurrent states when speculation and structured output were combined. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- llama.cpp needed partial `seq_rm` for recurrent (GDN) layers and a fix for recurrent state save/load with partial rollback before MTP could merge. — [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673)
- SGLang v0.5.16: "ReplaySSM Ring Spec-Verify" cut speculative scratch for linear-attention layers from 11.5 GB to 1.8 GB per GPU (6.4x) on a 35B hybrid MoE. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)

### Inferences
- On a bandwidth-bound device at batch 1, the weight stream is read once per step regardless of the number of verify rows (the A10G study found the multi-row GEMM no faster or slower than the single-row GEMV near the bandwidth limit), so verify rows are cheap in time until compute or KV attention becomes the bound. That is why depth 1-3 with a native head wins and why deep trees do not: per-position acceptance decays fast (70% to under 10% by position 7), while each extra row still costs arithmetic and KV-scan work. Energy is not reported by any of these sources; extra rows that are rejected are pure energy loss even when wall time is flat.
- The transferable engine idea is the measured-cost-table policy (argmax of expected accepted tokens over measured step cost), not any specific drafter. A single-device engine can profile its own step cost for 1..K rows at several context lengths (cost depends on context because the KV scan scales with rows x context) and pick depth per step. An energy-per-token variant only needs the denominator swapped from time to joules.
- Rollback for recurrent/hybrid layers is the recurring correctness hazard; every engine that added MTP had to add state checkpoint/partial rollback first.
- Retrieval drafting (suffix tree / prompt lookup) is worth having only for edit- and tool-heavy agent traffic; on general chat it is a slowdown (0.88x measured).
- Vocabulary pruning of a draft head saves compute but loses acceptance on multilingual/RAG traffic; a frequency-ranked subset with full-vocabulary verification is safe for correctness but not for speed in those domains.

### Gaps
- No source reports energy per token for any speculative method; all report time.
- No published acceptance rates from real production chat traffic; the closest are SPEED-Bench (curated public data) and TraceSpec (replayed agent traces, abstract only).
- EAGLE 3.1 acceptance lengths, and DFlash/DSpark acceptance lengths by context length up to 128k, were not found in the pages read.
- The hardware-aware speedup formula for unified memory (Bielik paper) was not extracted (abstract only).
- No Metal/Apple numbers were posted for llama.cpp's MTP path in the PR.
- Dynamic draft-tree shaping (EAGLE-2 style trees) on MTP heads: TensorRT-LLM release notes mention "dynamic-tree MTP decoding" for one model family, with no measurements found — [TensorRT-LLM releases](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-LLM/releases).

## 2. KV cache: quantization, compression/eviction, tiering, sharing

### Takeaway
The production consensus in 2026 is FP8 KV with attention computed directly in FP8 (2x capacity, near-zero loss when accumulation is done carefully), 4-bit storage as the practical floor (1-4 points loss, real latency cost when it must be dequantized), and 3-bit as unsafe at 128k-256k. Token eviction remains workload-fragile and breaks under multi-turn reuse unless it is query-agnostic; tiering KV to host RAM and SSD and session-aware prefix retention are the engine features that actually shipped, and hybrid (recurrent) models need state checkpoints rather than per-token caches.

### Cited Findings

Quantized KV with attention on the quantized data
- vLLM FP8 KV (April 22, 2026): `--kv-cache-dtype fp8` stores KV in FP8 (e4m3) and runs both attention matmuls (QK and score-V) in FP8. Default scales are per-tensor and uncalibrated (scale 1.0); per-attention-head scales with calibration via LLM-Compressor are supported; per-token scales are not described. — [vLLM blog: FP8 KV cache](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)
- Accuracy pitfall found and fixed: needle-in-a-haystack at 128k scored 91% in BF16, 13% with the original FP8 FlashAttention-3 kernel, 89% after a two-level FP32 accumulation fix. — [vLLM blog: FP8 KV cache](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)
- Long-context quality after the fix (MRCR AUC recovery): Llama-3.3-70B up to 128k 97-98%; Qwen3-30B-A3B up to 256k about 94% (BF16 weights) / 98% (FP8 weights); Qwen3.5-27B up to 1M matches baseline. Reasoning tasks lose at most 1-2 points. One MLA model showed a consistent downward shift uncalibrated, where calibration is advised. — [vLLM blog: FP8 KV cache](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)
- Speed: single-request inter-token-latency slope vs context fell to 54% of BF16 on Llama-3.1-8B (break-even about 7k tokens of context); gpt-oss-20b 80% slope, break-even 22.1k, improved to 71% and 7.7k by keeping sliding-window layers in BF16 (`--kv-cache-dtype-skip-layers sliding_window`). Below about 7k tokens BF16 can be slightly faster. Failure case: head_dim 256 models get slower prefill (TTFT quadratic coefficient about 1.6x). — [vLLM blog: FP8 KV cache](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)
- SGLang release notes list MXFP8 KV writes and an NVFP4 KV cache entry (v0.5.21, Oct 2026; the NVFP4 line was truncated in the page read). — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)

Low-bit KV storage (dequantize before attention)
- TurboQuant study in vLLM (May 11, 2026): compresses KV storage to 3-4 bits and dequantizes to BF16 for attention. Qwen3-30B-A3B up to 256k AUC: BF16 45.8%, FP8 43.1%, k8v4 43.0%, 4-bit 42.3%, k3v4 33.5%, 3-bit 31.2% (losses concentrate at 128k-256k). Llama-3.3-70B up to 64k: 4-bit variants hold about 52% AUC; 3-bit variants lose up to 8 points at 64k. Reasoning: FP8 and k8v4 recover over 98%, 4-bit about 96%, 3-bit variants lose about 20 points on one model. Latency overhead vs BF16: FP8 negligible; TurboQuant +10-60% (Qwen3-30B) and +10-68% (Llama-70B); offline throughput 66-80% of BF16. Capacity: FP8 2x, k8v4 2.4x, 4-bit up to 3.4x. Recommendation: FP8 default; 4-bit only under memory pressure or on edge devices; avoid 3-bit. — [vLLM blog: TurboQuant study](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-05-11-turboquant)
- Independent benchmark (arXiv 2607.05399, May 2026; Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B, LongBench-style): compression ratio alone is a poor predictor of end-to-end performance; KIVI at 4-bit gives the most stable quality; SnapKV gives the best long-context throughput; CaM (merging) is highly workload-sensitive. Abstract only (no per-length numbers extracted). — [Benchmarking KV-cache optimizations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05399)
- Community practice on Apple silicon: 4-bit and 8-bit KV cache options exposed in an MLX server; llama.cpp users run q4_0 KV (one PR benchmark used "KV q4_0"). — [MTPLX releases](https://github.com/youssofal/MTPLX/releases); [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673)

Eviction / pruning / merging (KIVI, GEAR, H2O, SnapKV, PyramidKV line and successors)
- Background benchmark (2024): 10+ approaches over seven categories of long-context tasks. — [KV Cache Compression, But What Must We Give in Return?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01527)
- KVFundaBench (Feb 2025): degradation ranges from 1% to 40% depending on task; long-context generation tasks are especially sensitive. — [KVFundaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01941v2)
- KVzip (NeurIPS 2025 oral): query-agnostic eviction scored by having the model reconstruct its context; 3-4x smaller KV and about 2x lower attention latency without degradation on QA, retrieval, math, code; compress once at prefill, reuse across queries; reusing a cache compressed for the first query significantly degrades SnapKV. Retraining: none. — [KVzip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416)
- Cost of KVzip: compression overhead is 2.1-2.2x the plain-prefill time at 4K-128K (measured by a follow-up). — [EchoPress](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.00412)
- A matched-budget audit (2026) found the analytically query-agnostic Expected Attention still loses to a simple keep-start-plus-recent baseline on its grid. — [How Query Visibility Changes KV-Cache Compression Rankings](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11942)
- 2026 successors (preprints, self-reported): VarRate (training-free variable-rate; claims no accuracy advantage over KVzip under matched settings) — [VarRate](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.15498); TaskPress (task-guided query-agnostic pruning) — [TaskPress](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03276); AlignPred (error-controlled compression via alignment and predictive coding; near-native LongBench-Pro quality over a range of storage rates) — [AlignPred](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17970); RIFT (KV compression for draft models; retains 96.4% / 89.9% of full-KV scores on LongBench v1 / v2 at an 8K budget) — [RIFT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.31098)

Paged and tiered KV (RAM to SSD)
- KVSwap (Nov 2025, Jetson Orin AGX 64 GB unified memory, NVMe 1.8 GB/s and eMMC 250 MB/s): full KV on disk, written layer by layer at prefill; RAM holds a low-rank K cache (SVD adapter) used only to predict which entries matter, a rolling buffer and a FIFO reuse buffer; attention scores estimated from low-rank keys pick top-M groups of G consecutive tokens (G=4 NVMe, G=8 eMMC), prefetched for layer i while layer i-1 computes. At 32K: KV budgets of 1/13 (about 310 MiB) and 1/34 (about 120 MiB) of the full cache; RULER loss -2.6% (NVMe, relaxed) to -5.6% (tight); LongBench -0.6% to -2.4%; reasoning -1.8% to -10.0%. Throughput 6.9 tok/s at 16K batch 1 on NVMe; up to 1.8x (NVMe) and 4.1x (eMMC) over the strongest offloading baseline. Retraining: none; offline calibration under 30 minutes. This is lossy (top-M selection). — [KVSwap](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11907v2)
- vLLM tiered KV offloading (available since v0.22; blog Sep 10, 2026): GPU -> host DRAM (LRU/ARC) -> filesystem/NVMe, object storage, or peer; fixed-size chunks (default one chunk = one block, `blocks_per_chunk` to enlarge I/O); async DMA and separate read/write thread pools; scheduler gets RETRY while a chunk is promoted. Storage tier more than doubles throughput once host RAM is also full (multi-turn 12K + 4K x 8 rounds); storage has higher latency than RAM. Lossless. — [vLLM blog: tiered KV offloading](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-10-tiered-kv-offloading)
- SGLang HiCache: L3 storage can be attached/detached on a running server (v0.5.19); HiSparse keeps KV in CPU memory with EAGLE and MTP support (v0.5.22). — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Apple silicon: an MLX server keeps a hot RAM tier and a cold SSD tier of block-based KV (safetensors), with prefix sharing and copy-on-write, restoring a matching prefix from disk after a restart; no independent benchmark found, and a commenter notes SSD bandwidth is far below RAM. — [oMLX](https://github.com/jundot/omlx)
- vllm-metal (Sep 2026) lists SSD-backed KV offload as still being matured; it reports oMLX with SSD offload leading at concurrency 1 on one 27B agent benchmark. — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0)
- LiteRT-LM supports saving and restoring large KV states across sessions to skip prefill on returning sessions. — [Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/blazing-fast-on-device-genai-with-litert-lm/)

Prefix sharing, radix trees, recurrent-state snapshots
- llama.cpp host-memory prompt cache (merged Oct 2025): prompt states stored in RAM act as "extra slots"; prefix similarity decides when to hot-swap one into the context; `--cache-ram`; default context checkpoints raised from 3 to 8. — [llama.cpp PR #16391](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/16391)
- llama.cpp hybrid-model pain points (2026 issue digests): every request forced full prompt reprocessing for a hybrid MoE family (Feb 2026); slot save/restore to disk silently fails to preserve prompt reuse on hybrid/recurrent models because the on-disk format omits context checkpoints. — [llama.cpp weekly digest, Feb 2026](https://buttondown.com/weekly-project-news/archive/weekly-github-report-for-llamacpp-february-15/); [llama.cpp weekly digest, Aug 2026](https://buttondown.com/weekly-project-news/archive/weekly-github-report-for-llamacpp-august-04-2026-1387/)
- Marconi (MLSys 2025; background-to-2025): prefix caching for hybrid models; recurrent states are large and only hit on exact matches, so it admits at most two state checkpoints per sequence (at radix-tree branch points), stores state at chunk boundaries, and uses FLOP-aware eviction; 4.5-34.4x higher token hit rate than vLLM/SGLang extended to hybrids. — [Marconi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19379)
- Sparse prefix caching (May 2026): store exact recurrent states at a sparse set of positions chosen by a dynamic program over the distribution of overlap depths; resume from the deepest checkpoint and recompute the gap exactly; biggest gains at low checkpoint budgets. Abstract only. — [Sparse Prefix Caching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05219)
- ReplaySSM (2026): cache recurrent-layer inputs rather than state to cut state traffic during decode; complementary to prefix caching. — [ReplaySSM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.13778)
- SGLang: unified radix tree is default for all models (v0.5.19); branching-point caching for sliding-window components raised token hit rate from 43.8% to 60.8% and cut mean TTFT 1.57 s -> 1.07 s (v0.5.20); session-aware radix cache protects prefixes an active session references (v0.5.17); prefix cache has a Rust core by default (v0.5.21). — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- vLLM AgentX work: one block pool shared across attention types (allocated on demand), session-aware prefix retention combining interval checkpoints with Marconi-style selective retention for prefixes seen twice. — [vLLM blog: AgentX](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx)
- vllm-metal: experimental `align` mode saves recurrent state at block boundaries for hybrid models. — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0)

### Inferences
- For a bandwidth-bound decode at 16k-128k, FP8/INT8 KV with attention computed in the 8-bit domain is the only option here that is both near-lossless and faster (it halves bytes read per step). 4-bit storage that is dequantized first saves memory but costs 10-68% latency on GPUs; whether it is a win on a unified-memory device depends on whether a kernel can do attention directly on 4-bit data, which no source here demonstrates.
- The 13% -> 89% needle result shows the accuracy risk is in the accumulation precision of the attention kernel, not in the 8-bit storage itself. Any engine writing its own quantized-attention kernel should test retrieval at 128k, not perplexity.
- Mixed precision by layer type (skip sliding-window layers) is free speed for hybrid-attention models: small caches gain nothing from quantization and pay the fixed overhead.
- Token eviction is a poor default for a multi-turn single-user engine: query-aware methods (SnapKV family) break on reuse, and the robust query-agnostic one (KVzip) costs about 2x prefill time. A keep-start-plus-recent baseline is a hard-to-beat fallback.
- Hybrid models change prefix caching from "per-token KV" to "sparse exact state checkpoints plus recompute"; checkpoint placement at chunk boundaries and at branch points is the general recipe.

### Gaps
- Per-token (as opposed to per-tensor or per-head) scales for KV quantization: not described in any engine source read.
- No quality numbers at 32k-128k for GEAR, H2O or PyramidKV specifically were retrieved; 2026 benchmarks used KIVI, TurboQuant, SnapKV, CaM. PyramidKV-specific 2026 results were not found.
- No measured SSD-tier KV results on Apple silicon from an independent source.
- Energy cost of KV tiering to SSD (SSD power vs recompute) is not reported anywhere found.
- [LeanK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.02215) (learnable K-cache channel pruning, 2025) was surfaced by title only; not read.

## 3. Attention kernels for long-context decode and prefill

### Takeaway
Decode attention at long context is a bandwidth problem solved by splitting the context dimension across workers and merging partial softmax results exactly (flash-decoding / split-K; LeanAttention's unequal splits), by reading fewer bytes (8-bit KV computed in place), and by packing variable-length requests instead of padding. 2026 engine work is mostly specialization: length-aware split-K paths for single-token decode and small verify blocks, windowed attention for draft heads, and graph capture of prefill.

### Cited Findings
- Background: LeanAttention (Microsoft, 2024; MLSys 2025) treats the online-softmax rescaling as an associative reduction so partial attention results from unequal context splits can be merged exactly; reports 2.6x average latency speedup over FlashAttention-2 and up to 8.33x at 512k context in the decode phase; decode at long context is bound by memory bandwidth and capacity. Self-reported. FlashDecoding's fixed number of splits K suffers load imbalance and reduction overhead that scales with K. — [LeanAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10480); [MLSys 2025 slides](https://mlsys.org/media/mlsys-2025/Slides/3269.pdf)
- A production split-K decode kernel: each thread block processes one split-K partition for one (batch, head) pair and writes partial softmax statistics that a separate reduce kernel merges. — [Modular MAX mha_decoding docs](https://docs.modular.com/max/api/kernels/nn/attention/gpu/mha/mha_decoding/)
- ExecuTorch (Aug 2026): "length-aware split-K FlashDecoding++ paths optimize single-token decode and small DFlash verification blocks" on CUDA; on Apple silicon, attention, KV updates, RMSNorm, RoPE and quantized linear ops are lowered to MLX-native or custom Metal kernels. Only 13 of that model's 52 layers are global attention (39 sliding-window), which is what makes 128K+ practical on edge devices. — [PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/fast-ondevice-agentic-ai-with-executorch/)
- SGLang v0.5.19: a persistent Lean attention kernel on AMD accelerators gives up to 1.52x throughput. Vendor. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Attention over quantized caches: FP8 QK and score-V matmuls on FP8 KV need two-level FP32 accumulation on one backend (FlashAttention-3) but not on another (FlashInfer); with head_dim 256 the fix makes prefill slower (about 1.6x TTFT quadratic coefficient), with an open proposal to accumulate every N steps. Query quantization was moved into a fusable torch op. — [vLLM blog: FP8 KV cache](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-22-fp8-kvcache)
- Packed variable-length attention on Metal: vllm-metal replaces mlx-lm attention with a paged varlen Metal kernel (ported from vLLM's unified Triton kernel); queries packed into [total_q, H, D] with cu_seqlens, KV in fixed-size pages via per-request block tables, so prefill and decode run in one forward. Mixed-length batch (one 3,000-token + seven 430-token requests): mlx_lm 10.99 s, llama.cpp 5.33 s, packed kernel 3.64 s; a padded engine slowed 143% going from uniform to mixed lengths. Vendor. — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0)
- Windowed draft-decode attention for EAGLE and MTP drafts (SGLang v0.5.21); IndexShare MTP cuts draft-step cost up to 1.9x at long context for sparse-attention models (v0.5.15); draft-extend logits pruned to selected rows +2.5% (v0.5.20). — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Prefill: SGLang "breakable CUDA graph" is the default capture path (v0.5.15), prefill capture default raised to the chunk size capped at 8192 (v0.5.23), mixed chunk prefill works with speculative decoding (v0.5.19). — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Chunked prefill scheduling: capping long prefills at 512 tokens per step (`--long-prefill-token-threshold`) raised total tokens per GPU-second by up to 93% and P90 interactivity about 2.3x on an agentic workload, at the cost of TTFT for the long request itself. — [vLLM blog: AgentX](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx)
- Apple silicon decode at long context from a community MLX server after a "flash-decoding route" (M5-class GPUs, macOS 26.2+ only): one MoE model 53.2 -> 68.4 tok/s at 16k, 47.5 -> 60.9 at 100k, 32.2 -> 44.2 at 206k; a 27B model 20.5 -> 30.9 tok/s at 88k. Self-reported. — [MTPLX releases](https://github.com/youssofal/MTPLX/releases)
- KV-scan cost model: per-iteration decode time = weight-streaming time + a KV-scan term that grows with number of sequences and context length. — [tokens-per-watt analytical paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17280v1)
- KV layout pitfalls on Metal: pre-allocating the KV cache in MLX gave no generation speedup and raised first-token latency (260 ms -> 276-285 ms) and memory (+0.25 to +0.52 GB); a lazily-backed large virtual KV buffer via no-copy Metal buffers did not save physical memory on a 27B hybrid model at 64K (19.8 vs 19.9 GB). — [OptMLX](https://atomgradient.github.io/OptMLX); [demand-paging post (corrected Sep 2026)](https://rockyrunnr.github.io/posts/demand-paging-fails-apple-silicon-gpu)

### Inferences
- The exact-merge property (associative softmax reduction) is what lets a decode kernel parallelize across the context dimension with any split shape; on a unified-memory GPU this is the route to reading each K/V byte once per step at full bandwidth, with all heads of a layer handled in one pass over the page.
- The measured decline with context on Apple silicon (68 -> 61 -> 44 tok/s from 16k to 206k) is consistent with the weight-stream + KV-scan model: the KV term only dominates past roughly 100k for MoE models with small active weights, so halving KV bytes (8-bit) matters most beyond that point, and earlier for dense models with large KV per token.
- Packed (unpadded) batches matter even for "a few concurrent requests": padding to the longest request more than doubled wall time in the mixed-length test.

### Gaps
- No source describes head-batching strategy or cache-line/page layout for KV explicitly tuned to unified memory; the vllm-metal post gives the layout but not bandwidth utilization.
- Blocked prefill attention (tiling for cache reuse) on Apple GPUs: no measurements found beyond one M5 prefill chart without numbers.
- No independent replication of LeanAttention's numbers.

## 4. Decode-loop and scheduler design

### Takeaway
Both major server engines converged on the same loop design in 2025-2026: persistent per-request state on the device, inputs for step N+1 built on the device while step N runs, sampling on the device without a full softmax, outputs copied back asynchronously, and the whole draft-verify loop captured as one graph. Gains are largest at batch 1 with small models and with speculative decoding, which is exactly the single-user regime.

### Cited Findings
- vLLM Model Runner V2 (March 24, 2026; opt-in as of v0.18.0): each live request gets a stable row in a fixed-size state table; a gather builds ordered inputs each step; kernels build input_ids, positions and sequence metadata directly on the GPU; scheduling and preparation of step N+1 overlap GPU execution of step N; aims for zero CPU-GPU synchronization; outputs move to CPU asynchronously on a separate stream; prep kernels consume rejection-sampling results directly, which is what makes async scheduling compatible with speculative decoding. Qwen3-0.6B: about 25K vs 16K output tok/s (+56.2%); a large model with MTP=1: 6.3% lower mean TPOT. Vendor. — [vLLM blog: Model Runner V2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2)
- GPU-side sampling in MRV2: Gumbel-Max kernel avoids materializing softmax with stateless in-kernel RNG; top-k logprobs found from logits first and logprobs computed only for selected candidates; an index-mapping indirection avoids expanding request state per logits row (helps speculative decoding). — [vLLM blog: Model Runner V2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2)
- SGLang: overlap scheduler dates to v0.4 (Dec 2024, background); Spec V2 "zero-overhead scheduling" for speculative decoding became default in v0.5.15 (July 2026) for about +11% end-to-end TPS; with overlap scheduling plus on-device sampling masks, decode throughput on an 8B model rose 17% at batch 1 and 52% at batch 64 (v0.5.20). Vendor. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Graph capture of the speculative loop: capturing the draft-extend graph gives about 1.5x decode throughput at batch size 1; acceptance-tensor preparation fused (SGLang v0.5.23). TensorRT-LLM reached the highest peak throughput in SPEED-Bench by running the whole draft-verify loop in one CUDA graph; vLLM showed slight host-communication overhead but more flexibility. — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases); [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)
- ExecuTorch captures decode into one CUDA graph (one submission instead of per-kernel launches), and serves multiple isolated conversations from one model load via per-session mutable-state rebinding; no cross-session prefix sharing, checkpointing or continuous batching yet (Aug 2026). — [PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/fast-ondevice-agentic-ai-with-executorch/)
- Continuous batching at small batch counts on Apple silicon: vLLM's scheduler, paged KV, chunked prefill and sampling on top of MLX/Metal; memory guard measures usage in a startup warmup, fixes the KV pool, caps MLX's buffer cache, and makes requests that do not fit wait for free pages. An earlier MLX-native server reported up to 4.3x aggregate throughput at 16 concurrent requests on an M4 Max and 21-87% higher throughput than llama.cpp for text models. Vendor / self-reported. — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0); [vllm-mlx paper, Jan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19139)
- Constrained decoding cost: XGrammar (background, Nov 2024) reports under 40 microseconds per token for JSON Schema/JSON CFG and under 200 microseconds for XML and Python DSL. A 2026 paper measures llguidance mask computation at 73-141 microseconds vs 5.4-9.5 for XGrammar on enum schemas with a 151K vocabulary, with llguidance faster to compile (lazy Earley). Another 2026 paper reports XGrammar at hundreds of milliseconds per token on some grammars and llguidance at 1-1.4 ms (conflicts with XGrammar's own numbers; treat with caution). — [XGrammar](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15100); [Trie automata paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12574); [Parser stack classification paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03065)
- JSONSchemaBench (2025): six constrained-decoding frameworks on 10K real-world schemas; end-to-end results are a better guide than per-token microbenchmarks. — [JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868v3)
- SGLang uses llguidance 1.7.6 as grammar backend (v0.5.16) and added decode-graph hooks for eager logits processing (v0.5.21). — [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- Time to first token: MTP reduced time to first output 10.0-14.2% in the A10G study; session-aware radix caching cut mean TTFT 1.57 s -> 1.07 s; SGLang startup: overlapped checkpoint staging during graph capture starts a 32B model 2.38x faster (35.6 s vs 84.8 s), contiguous-copy before host-to-device cut one model load from 545 s to 70 s. — [Beneath the Tokens](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35188); [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- vLLM lists a Sep 2026 post on cutting startup time with a Python bytecode flag and a Nov 2026 post on "high-interactivity decode with monokernels" for agents (titles only; not read). — [vLLM blog archive](https://vllm.ai/blog/archive)

### Inferences
- The MRV2 result on a 0.6B model (+56% from moving input prep to the device) shows host overhead is a first-order term whenever the model step is short. On a unified-memory device with a lazy-evaluation framework the analogue is: build the next step's graph inputs and submit them before reading back the sampled token, and never force a synchronization for sampling.
- Sampling without softmax (Gumbel-Max) and computing logprobs only for selected candidates are free wins for any engine with a large vocabulary; they also remove one full-vocabulary pass per step, which matters for energy.
- Grammar masks cost microseconds to about a millisecond per token on the CPU; at 20-70 tok/s on a laptop that is under 10% in the worst credible case, so the engine concern is not mask cost but keeping the mask application off the synchronization path (apply on device, compute mask on CPU during the model step).

### Gaps
- No published measurement of scheduler/host overhead as a share of step time for MLX- or Metal-based engines.
- No energy measurements for async scheduling or graph capture.
- Constrained decoding combined with speculative decoding: only bug-fix notes found, no cost data.

## 5. Weight formats and quantization at 3-5 bits

### Takeaway
4-bit with good scales remains the knee: K-quant 4-bit stays within noise of FP16 on an 8B model while 3-bit small variants lose about 5-6% relative; microscaling FP4 (MXFP4 at 4.25 bits, NVFP4 at 4.5) is the format hardware is being built for, and 2026 kernel work focuses on running it as INT8 matrix products. Evidence on vector quantization and principled per-layer bit allocation in shipping engines is thin.

### Cited Findings
- llama.cpp K-quants on Llama-3.1-8B (Jan 2026 study, CPU server; no imatrix/IQ variants tested): average of five benchmarks F16 69.47; Q4_K_M 69.15 (perplexity 7.56 vs 7.32); Q4_K_S 69.17; Q5_K_M 69.36; Q3_K_M 68.07; Q3_K_S 65.49 (perplexity 8.96, GSM8K 77.63 -> 68.31). Paper recommends Q4_K_S as balanced default and warns against aggressive 3-bit for math/instruction-heavy work; differences of about one point are within its noise. — [GGUF quantization study](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14277v1)
- Qwen2.5-32B on vLLM (Jan 2026, practitioner benchmark): HumanEval AWQ 51.8%, GGUF 51.8%, FP16 56.1%, GPTQ 46%; kernel choice dominates speed (same AWQ weights reach 741 tok/s with the Marlin kernel). — [Jarvis Labs benchmark](https://docs.jarvislabs.ai/blog/vllm-quantization-complete-guide-benchmarks)
- Format sizes: MXFP4 stores 32 weights in 17 bytes (4.25 bits per weight); llama.cpp's quantize tool measures Q4_K_M at 4.8944 bits per weight; community quality testing of MXFP4 vs Q4_K_M on the same model is described as thin. — [MXFP4 vs Q4_K_M guide](https://openclawdc.com/blog/mxfp4-vs-q4-k-m/)
- Integer-exact microscaling (Nov 2026; Apple M5 GPU "Neural Accelerators" via INT8 x INT8): a row of MXFP4 weights can be written as one INT8 vector times one power-of-two scale when its block exponents span at most three, turning a linear layer into a single INT8 GEMM with one rescale per output; 97.9%-99.97% of rows in seven models already satisfy this; constrained files remain standard MXFP4. A variant with integer block multipliers (16-wide blocks, 4.26 bits per weight) is more accurate than NVFP4 under its standard scale rule and within 0.06 perplexity of NVFP4 with scale search; GSM8K 94.8% vs 91.0% for MLX's MXFP4 with every matrix quantized. Prefill vs MLX: 1.34-1.74x dense, 1.10-1.57x on two MoE models up to 35B. A search summary of the same paper says it loses more than every llama.cpp type on the two MoEs tested and that Q4_K_M (about 5 bits) is more accurate. Abstract only; no energy numbers. Retraining: none (re-quantization). — [Integer-Exact Microscaling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00486)
- Mixed precision: MXFP6 and mixed MXFP4-MXFP6 generally give better accuracy than MXFP4 alone (AMD post). — [AMD ROCm blog](https://rocm.blogs.amd.com/software-tools-optimization/mxfp4-mxfp6-quantization/README.html)
- Native MXFP4 models and fine-tuning: native MXFP4 precision has not yet proven stable for fine-tuning; quantization-aware training is a separate recipe (needs training). — [NVIDIA developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/fine-tuning-gpt-oss-for-accuracy-and-performance-with-quantization-aware-training)
- K-quants as an execution format: ExecuTorch maps Q4_K/Q5_K/Q6_K to packed INT4/5/6 with dot-product GEMV kernels on CUDA and repacked or fused Metal kernels on MLX; at repack time it merges adjacent sub-blocks whose scale and min are identical into larger groups (up to 128) whenever the merge is lossless. — [PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/fast-ondevice-agentic-ai-with-executorch/)
- Kernel library for many formats (Oct 2026, ships with vLLM 0.22.0): one standard internal representation for groupwise INT4, MXFP4, NVFP4, and W-n-A16 with INT1-3 and FP4-FP8 weights; kernels JIT-compiled and cached (compile 23.35 s -> 1.82 s); W4A8-MXFP4 uses a fast MXFP4 -> INT8/FP8 dequantization path; small-batch dense W4A16 about 3-4x a BLAS baseline. No accuracy results. Vendor. — [vLLM blog: Humming](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-21-humming)
- Vocabulary head: pruned-vocabulary draft heads are covered in section 1 (FR-Spec, EAGLE-3's 32k subset and its 22% miss rate on multilingual text). — [FR-Spec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14856); [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)
- Embedding/head precision matters for speed: in a community benchmark of a 120B MXFP4 model, two builds differed significantly in speed, traced to different precision for embedding layers; on the 20B model MXFP4 and Q8_0 decode at similar speed. — [Framework community thread](https://community.frame.work/t/will-the-ai-max-395-128gb-be-able-to-run-gpt-oss-120b/73280/26)
- An MLX quantized-layer detail: `QuantizedLinear.weight.dtype` reports uint32 (packed storage) rather than working precision; code that keys on it mis-detects precision. — [OptMLX](https://atomgradient.github.io/OptMLX)

### Inferences
- For a bandwidth-bound decode, bits per weight is speed and energy: 4.25-bit MXFP4 reads about 13% fewer bytes than a 4.89-bit K-quant. The open question is accuracy at equal bits, and the one direct comparison found is mixed (better than MLX's MXFP4 and close to NVFP4, but worse than llama.cpp K-quants on MoEs per the search summary).
- The "one INT8 GEMM per layer, scales hoisted out of the inner loop" idea is general: any block-scaled format whose row can be re-expressed with a single scale lets the kernel use the fastest integer datapath and makes results bit-exact and reproducible.
- Lossless merging of identical adjacent sub-block scales is a free repack-time optimization for any block-scaled format.

### Gaps
- No 2025-2026 source read gives measured accuracy and inference cost for vector quantization / codebook (palettized) formats (for example trellis or additive codebooks) in a shipping local engine; searches returned nothing usable.
- No source read quantifies imatrix vs non-imatrix K-quants in 2026 beyond a practitioner statement that imatrix tends to be better at the same bit width.
- No principled per-layer bit-allocation study tied to a shipping engine was found; only the MXFP4/MXFP6 mixing post.
- "Bridging the Gap Between Promise and Performance for Microscaling FP4 Quantization" was surfaced by title only — [arXiv 2509.23202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.23202).
- Two-stage / candidate-vocabulary output heads for the target model itself (not the drafter): no source found.

## 6. Memory-system techniques

### Takeaway
On Apple silicon the binding fact is that GPU-visible buffers are wired, so the page-cache tricks that work on CPU inference (mmap, demand paging) do not give larger-than-RAM inference on the GPU; the working designs keep a bounded wired pool and page explicitly (expert slots loaded with pread, KV groups prefetched from SSD), with throughput falling smoothly as the pool shrinks. Engines otherwise defend against memory pressure with admission control: measure at warmup, fix the pools, refuse or queue what does not fit.

### Cited Findings
- llama.cpp loading modes: mmap is the default (demand paged via the OS page cache); server options include mmap, mlock, mmap+mlock and direct I/O; the loader has an O_DIRECT path on Linux with reads aligned to filesystem block size and a fallback to buffered I/O. — [llama.cpp tuning guide](https://medium.com/@michael.hannecke/tuning-llama-cpp-on-apple-silicon-843f37a6c3dc); [llama.cpp discussion #19883](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/19883)
- Apple silicon practice (practitioner guide): `--mlock` only with headroom; if model + KV + OS exceeds about 70% of unified memory, mlock makes the rest of the system thrash; raising `iogpu.wired_limit_mb` is called the single most important tuning step; Metal exposes a recommended working set of about 75% of RAM. — [llama.cpp tuning guide](https://medium.com/@michael.hannecke/tuning-llama-cpp-on-apple-silicon-843f37a6c3dc)
- mmap in MLX (exploratory, M1 Max 32 GB): zero-copy mmap of safetensors with Metal buffer sharing improved load time for some models (2.572 s -> 0.125 s for an 8B 8-bit; 4.34x for a 14B 4-bit) but slowed small ones (0.77x), left generation speed unchanged, and in some runs doubled peak memory (4.72 GB -> 8.82 GB) because the mapped region coexisted with materialized copies; conclusion: MLX's pread-based loader is already efficient. — [OptMLX](https://atomgradient.github.io/OptMLX)
- MoE expert paging on Metal (llama.cpp RFC + proof of concept, May 19, 2026): wired memory on Apple silicon "can't be compressed or swapped", so each MoE layer gets a pool of N expert slots in Metal shared memory; a Metal kernel publishes needed expert IDs, a CPU sidecar thread loads missing experts with pread from the GGUF and signals a shared event, then the GPU runs the expert matmul against the pool; plain LRU (segmented LRU gave no improvement); exact results (no zero/random fallback); requires `--no-mmap` and `--no-warmup`; constraint ubatch x experts-used <= slots. On an M3 Pro 36 GB, 30B-A3B at Q6_K: vanilla about 27.2 GiB wired, 38.1 tok/s; 96 slots 21.6 GiB, 30 tok/s; 64 slots 16.1 GiB, 27.5 tok/s; 32 slots 10.6 GiB, 22.1 tok/s; 8 slots 6.3 GiB, 12 tok/s; 13 tok/s on an M1 Pro 16 GB. Benefit depends on expert usage skew (one newer model family spreads usage more evenly). No maintainer response on the page. — [llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324)
- Related data in the same thread: on an M1 Air 16 GB (CPU), text-calibrated expert pinning reached 42% hit rate vs 6% for random profiles; pipelined I/O gave up to 1.3x at 75% hits; on Windows/CUDA streaming reached 0.43-0.75 tok/s, and pinning 24 GiB on a 32 GB machine collapsed to 0.12 tok/s. — [llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324)
- Lazily backed Metal buffers: a large virtual allocation wrapped with a no-copy Metal buffer reduced resident memory for small models but gave no saving for a 27B hybrid model at 64K (19.8 vs 19.9 GB); "no-copy" means reuse of storage, not lazy physical commitment; the author retracted a general claim that GPUs have no page-fault handling and moved to a grow-and-copy KV buffer. — [demand-paging post (corrected Sep 2026)](https://rockyrunnr.github.io/posts/demand-paging-fails-apple-silicon-gpu)
- Weights larger than RAM (research line): background, LLM in a Flash (Apple, 2023) keeps parameters in flash and loads on demand using windowing and row-column bundling — [LLM in a Flash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11514); ActiveFlow (2025) swaps active weights between DRAM and flash based on top-k activation sparsity for modern (non-ReLU) models — [ActiveFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08378); FlexInfer (2025) uses asynchronous prefetching, balanced memory locking and flexible tensor preservation, up to 12.5x over prior offloading under limited memory — [FlexInfer](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03777v1); Lever (2026) notes flash provides much lower effective bandwidth than DRAM (typically about 5 GB/s), making flash-to-DRAM transfer the decode bottleneck, and uses speculation to amortize it — [Lever](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16786)
- Offload penalty on a discrete-GPU machine: a 70B Q4_K_M with CPU offload on an RTX 5090 runs at 2.3 tok/s vs 76.1 tok/s for a fully GPU-resident smaller quant of another model (paper has internal inconsistencies in these figures). — [Silicon Showdown](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00519v1)
- Admission control under memory pressure: vllm-metal sets a budget, measures at warmup, fixes the KV pool, caps MLX's buffer cache, queues requests that do not fit — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0); a community MLX server rewrote its memory guard twice in Oct 2026 after over-refusing on 8/16/32 GB Macs and still refuses long prompts when free memory is low — [MTPLX releases](https://github.com/youssofal/MTPLX/releases)
- Footprint tricks on phones: LiteRT-LM runs a 2.58 GB model in a 607 MB physical footprint on Apple mobile CPUs using weight caching, keeps per-layer embeddings out of memory, and loads encoders on demand. Vendor. — [Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/blazing-fast-on-device-genai-with-litert-lm/)
- KV on disk for long context: KVSwap (section 2) — [KVSwap](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11907v2)

### Inferences
- The expert-slot design is the general pattern for larger-than-RAM inference on a wired-memory GPU: bounded wired pool, explicit pread (which also avoids holding a second copy in the page cache if the file is opened without caching), an LRU, and a GPU-CPU handshake per layer. Its throughput curve (38 -> 30 -> 22 -> 12 tok/s as wired memory goes 27 -> 22 -> 11 -> 6 GiB) is the reference trade-off; the cost is a synchronization point per MoE layer, which conflicts with the async loop design in section 4 unless the needed experts are predicted a layer ahead (as KVSwap does for KV groups).
- Double buffering is a real hazard: the MLX mmap experiment doubled peak memory when mapped and materialized copies coexisted. Either map and use in place, or read with uncached I/O into wired buffers; never both.
- Admission control (refuse or queue) is what shipping Mac engines do about memory pressure; none of the sources describes reacting to OS memory-pressure notifications by shrinking pools at run time.

### Gaps
- No source read documents page-cache double buffering or uncached-read behaviour (F_NOCACHE) for model loading on macOS with measurements.
- No authoritative description of exactly when Metal wires shared/no-copy buffers; the one detailed post explicitly says it did not establish the physical commitment point.
- No energy data for SSD-backed weights or KV (SSD read energy per token vs recompute).
- How engines respond to OS kills for wired-memory overuse: not covered by any source read.

## 7. Energy

### Takeaway
Published laptop/SoC energy numbers are not comparable across papers: most use the OS's modelled rail estimates (CPU+GPU, sometimes ANE; DRAM usually excluded), wall power is often estimated rather than metered, and cross-vendor comparisons mix boundaries. The robust findings are structural: decode is bandwidth-bound and largely insensitive to clock frequency (so lower frequency/power caps save energy during decode), and tokens per joule falls roughly as 1/context because the KV scan grows while power stays flat. No engine-vs-engine energy comparison on the same Mac with a stated boundary was found.

### Cited Findings
Methodology
- A benchmarking harness (Stanford) on Apple silicon uses `powermetrics` (needs root) for separate CPU, GPU and ANE rails; whole-chip "soc" metrics sum the three per sample; DRAM energy is not addressed; energy is split into prefill and decode phases and per-query energy derived from a cumulative counter since baseline; GPU-only fields understate backends that use the ANE; metrics collected before its switch to the SoC basis are not comparable with later ones. On NVIDIA it uses NVML's cumulative millijoule counter plus RAPL for CPU. — [Intelligence-per-Watt platform support](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/intelligence-per-watt/benchmarking/platform-support/)
- Sources disagree on what the Apple estimates cover: one preprint says CPU+GPU package power as estimated by IOReport, excluding DRAM and peripherals; a blog says whole-SoC including memory controller and I/O. Wall power is commonly estimated from specifications rather than metered. — [GreenBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28667); [tokens-per-joule blog](https://dev.to/dilberx/why-tokens-per-joule-matters-more-than-tokens-per-second-1o06)
- GreenBench (M4 Pro 48 GB, Ollama, five 3-9B models): reports CPU+GPU package power of about 0.47 W during sustained inference vs 8-12 W total system power, and concludes 30-40x better energy per token than datacenter GPUs for single-user use; per-token figures from a search summary: 4.4 mJ/token (3B) to 11.6 mJ/token (9B) at package level. The 0.47 W package figure is implausibly low for sustained GPU inference and the system figure comes from specifications, so treat absolute values as unreliable. — [GreenBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28667)
- Cross-platform comparison (TensorRT-LLM v1.1.0 vs MLX v0.30.6 vs llama.cpp): NVML counters polled at 100 ms vs `powermetrics` SoC power; claims up to 23x more tokens per joule for an M3 Ultra vs an RTX 5090 on a 1.5B model; energy plots omitted "for brevity"; boundaries differ (SoC vs GPU board). — [Silicon Showdown](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00519v1)
- Other single-setup data points: M1 Pro 2.42 tokens/J vs RTX 3080 0.90 tokens/J on identical workloads (blog, single run) — [tokens-per-joule blog](https://dev.to/dilberx/why-tokens-per-joule-matters-more-than-tokens-per-second-1o06); an NVIDIA L4 estimated at 2.4x the energy per token of an M2 Pro, valid only for single-user edge use — [Bielik paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16368)

Structure of energy per token
- Analytical model (March 2026): decode time per iteration = weight streaming + KV scan growing with sequences x context; power follows a logistic curve in log2(concurrency) between idle and saturation; tokens per watt halves every time the context window doubles; same hardware and model ranges about 35.0 tok/W at 2K to 0.88 tok/W at 128K (about 40x); values are upper bounds (chunked prefill, bursts, memory pressure not modelled). It cites decode as 77-91% of inference time and largely bandwidth-bound, and a benchmark where energy per token rose about 3x from 2K to 10K prompts due to prefill. — [tokens-per-watt analytical paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17280v1)
- Bandwidth evidence on Apple silicon: dense 70B model 13.1 tok/s on M3 Ultra vs 5.1 on M4 Pro (about 2.5x, tracking the about 3x bandwidth ratio); an 80B MoE inverts this (52.3 tok/s on M4 Pro vs 49.1 on M3 Ultra), which the authors attribute (as hypothesis) to a shift from bandwidth-bound to latency-sensitive behaviour. — [Silicon Showdown](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00519v1)

Energy-aware scheduling
- GPU frequency-scaling characterization (2025, five models, four workloads): the decode phase is comparatively insensitive to frequency; combining workload-aware model selection with DVFS cut energy up to 87% in a case study. — [Characterizing LLM Inference Energy-Performance Tradeoffs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08219)
- GreenLLM (Aug 2025): separate prefill and decode frequency control with a feedback controller tracking tokens per second in decode; up to 34% less total energy than the default DVFS policy on A100s with few SLO misses. — [GreenLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16449v1)
- PALS (May 2026): GPU power caps as primary control; tokens/J vs cap and batch; communication-bound MoE models peak at around 200 W. — [PALS](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.21427v1)
- A 2026 survey notes clusters keep GPUs at high power while idle because wake-up overhead could hurt latency. — [Energy-Aware Computing in the Year 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24569)

Engine/backend comparisons with energy
- NPU path for MoE on Apple silicon (April 2026; prefill focus): static capacity tiers for experts, grouped expert execution, load-aware graph residency; 1.32-5.55x lower latency and 1.81-7.37x better energy efficiency vs baselines; a search summary states CoreML CPU, naive CoreML and another NPU framework consume 1.81-3.19x, 4.71-7.37x and 1.86-3.43x more energy per token respectively. Abstract only. — [NPUMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18788)
- Hardware power context: an M3 Ultra drew 138 W at 21.5 tok/s for the largest model in one wattage-based cost analysis. — [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/how-much-does-a-local-llm-actually-cost-to-run-i-measured-every-watt-on-apple-silicon/)

### Inferences
- For a bandwidth-bound decode, running the GPU faster than the memory system can feed it wastes energy; the server-side DVFS results (decode insensitive to frequency, 34% savings) suggest "pace to bandwidth" beats "race to idle" during decode, while prefill (compute-bound) favours running fast. No source tests this on a laptop SoC, where the engine cannot set frequency directly and can only shape load (batch rows, pacing, avoiding wasted work).
- Because tokens per joule falls with context, energy claims must state context length, phase split (prefill vs decode), measurement boundary and whether idle power is subtracted. A same-machine A/B with interleaved runs on SoC-rail energy is the defensible comparison; cross-vendor ratios (23x, 30-40x) are not.
- Every technique in sections 1-3 that reduces bytes read or steps executed per token (8-bit KV, fewer bits per weight, accepted draft tokens) should reduce energy roughly in proportion, while rejected draft rows and recomputed prefixes add energy without showing up in time-based benchmarks.

### Gaps
- No validation of `powermetrics`/IOReport estimates against a wall meter or external rail measurement for LLM workloads was found.
- No published 2025-2026 comparison of engines (MLX-based vs llama.cpp vs others) on the same Mac in joules per token with a stated boundary; the cross-platform paper omitted its energy plots.
- No study of race-to-idle vs paced decode on laptop SoCs; no study of DRAM energy during decode on unified memory.
- No energy numbers for speculative decoding, KV quantization, or SSD tiering.

## 9. Open problems flagged for local / edge inference

### Takeaway
The unsolved items researchers and engine authors point to are: speculative decoding that stays effective at long context and on real agent traces, larger-than-RAM operation on wired-memory GPUs without per-layer stalls, correct state rollback and caching for hybrid recurrent models, lossless or reliably bounded KV compression at 128k+, and trustworthy energy measurement.

### Cited Findings
- Drafters degrade outside their training context length and with unfamiliar chat templates/system prompts; aggregate acceptance hides declines within late agent actions; throughput falls at long context even when acceptance rises. — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557); [vLLM blog: EAGLE 3.1](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-05-26-eagle-3-1); [TraceSpec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41177)
- Existing speculative methods assume server accelerators and ignore long I/O latency, limited compute parallelism and irregular speculation execution on phones. — [Lever](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16786)
- On unified memory, verification "does not amortize as theory predicts" because draft and target are both bandwidth-bound. — [Bielik paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16368)
- Speculation memory overhead cancels the gain on small-memory machines (extra context/KV for the draft head; usable context shrinks). — [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673); [dev.to test](https://dev.to/rosgluk/qwen-36-27b-and-35b-mtp-vs-standard-on-16gb-gpu-42jd)
- Feature interactions are unfinished: MTP on Metal requires greedy sampling and disabling async scheduling and cannot combine with hybrid prefix caching; llama.cpp MTP requires one slot and partial rollback is broken for batch above 1; SGLang fixed wrong recurrent-state resume when speculation met structured output. — [vLLM blog: vllm-metal](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0); [llama.cpp PR #22673](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/22673); [SGLang releases](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases)
- KV compression: ratio does not predict end-to-end performance; workload-aware selection is needed; 1-40% task-dependent degradation; query-aware pruning breaks on multi-turn reuse; 3-bit KV fails at 128k-256k. — [KV benchmark 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05399); [KVFundaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01941v2); [KVzip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416); [vLLM blog: TurboQuant](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-05-11-turboquant)
- Larger-than-RAM on Apple GPUs: wired memory cannot be compressed or swapped; expert paging benefit depends on usage skew and newer models spread usage more evenly; lazy physical backing of Metal buffers was not achieved. — [llama.cpp discussion #23324](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/23324); [demand-paging post](https://rockyrunnr.github.io/posts/demand-paging-fails-apple-silicon-gpu)
- Flash bandwidth (about 5 GB/s) is far below DRAM, making flash-to-DRAM transfer the decode bottleneck for flash-resident models. — [Lever](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16786)
- Edge surveys: once the model is loaded, long-context prompts and multi-user concurrency quickly exhaust memory; core constraints are compute, memory capacity and memory bandwidth. — [Network Edge Inference for LLMs, Apr 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22906)
- NPUs: dynamic routing, irregular operators (top-k, scatter/gather) and many small kernels make MoE hard on shape-static NPUs. — [NPUMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18788)
- Energy reporting: tokens per watt is meaningless without context length; measurement boundaries differ between vendors. — [tokens-per-watt analytical paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17280v1); [Intelligence-per-Watt platform support](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/intelligence-per-watt/benchmarking/platform-support/)
- Benchmarks: synthetic random-token prompts overstate speculative throughput by about 23% and distort MoE expert routing. — [SPEED-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09557)

### Inferences
- Three gaps look open for a single-device engine to lead on, since no source covers them: (a) an energy-denominated verify-row policy (joules instead of seconds in the cost table), (b) attention computed directly on 4-bit KV without a dequantize pass, and (c) predictive, non-blocking expert/KV paging that keeps the async decode loop intact on a wired-memory GPU.
- Any benchmark of such an engine should use real prompts (not random tokens), report context length and phase split with energy, and test retrieval at the longest context for every KV or kernel change.

### Gaps
- No single 2026 survey dedicated to open problems in on-device inference was found; the list above is assembled from limitations sections and engine notes.
- The open-challenges sections of the edge surveys were not read in full.
